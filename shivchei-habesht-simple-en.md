## The Author's Introduction

I want to explain to the reader why I decided to write this book, so that he will not wonder at it. There are people who mock and say: "If you had kept silent, your silence would have been finer than your words." But there is no real claim in what they say; it is only mockery in another style.

God is my witness that I have no intention of boasting, or of looking great, or of making a name for myself in the world.

I will reveal a little of my reasons. I heard from the great rabbi, Rabbi Gedaliah, in the name of the pious Rabbi Shmeril, the preacher of the town of Verkhivka, words about the famous signs, wonders and miracles, and about how a person must hold on to faith.

I too have noticed it: from the time I became a man until I grew old, I see that because of our sins the miracles and wonders grow fewer from day to day. In earlier times it sometimes happened that a person lay in a faint, like a dead man, and afterward got up and came back to life, and told of the awesome things he had seen in the upper world. So it is told in the Gemara too ("I saw a clear world"), and in other stories in the Gemara.

For example, my father-in-law, Rabbi Alexander, told that in the town of Bershtat a certain man lay in such a faint. This was at the time when the sect of Shabbetai Tzvi was stirring. They showed this man places in books where some rabbis had gone wrong, rabbis who had almost been drawn after the sect, and they commanded him to tell them the true meaning of those places.

In his days, too, there were madmen who struck themselves with stones during the Torah reading. They revealed people's sins to them, and told about themselves for which sin they were being punished in the "hollow of the sling" (a punishment in which the soul is hurled from place to place, like a stone in a sling).

I remember that in my youth I studied in a village with a teacher. Poor people came to the cheder, and they were given food. Among them was a woman into whom an evil spirit had entered, and nobody knew of it. When the teacher began to study the Five Books of Moses with the children, he managed to study only two or three verses, and the evil spirit threw the woman to the ground. Her husband came and asked the teacher to stop studying, because the impure spirit in her could not bear anything holy. When the teacher stopped, the woman got up and sat down again in her place.

Because of all these things, many people repented, and faith grew stronger in the heart of every Jew.

But today, because of our sins, the righteous are few, faith has sunk very low, and much heresy has spread in the world. May God forgive it.

So I took the advice to write down the awesome stories I heard from truthful men. In each story I wrote from whom I heard it. Thank God, who gave me a good memory. I added nothing and took nothing away; it is all true, and nothing changed as I told it.

I wrote all this as a remembrance for my children and my grandchildren, to strengthen in them faith in God and in His Torah, faith in the righteous, and faith in everyone who draws himself close to God and to the Torah; and so that they will see how the Torah purifies the souls of those who study it, and how a person can reach high levels, as our Sages said: "A person should always hope and say: When will my deeds reach the deeds of my fathers Abraham, Isaac and Jacob?"

Therefore everyone who reads this should know: I did not write it in order to tell stories or to write history. I wrote it so that the reader would reflect with his mind on each and every deed, see in it the wonders of God, and learn from it a moral lesson for himself: to bind his heart to the fear of God, to faith in the sages, and to the power of the holy Torah.

I wanted to write after each story what moral lesson and what faith one learns from it. But I was afraid that I would not write it well enough, and so I left it to each reader to learn from the story according to his own level: the small one according to his smallness, and the great one according to his greatness.

And I said to myself: together with my children, perhaps I will merit to give this to the rest of the Jewish people as well. May God help me to be among those who bring merit to the Jewish people. I ask my descendants to give the book to everyone. And to everyone who reads it I will tell things from days of old, as it is written in Psalms: "That which we have heard and known, and our fathers have told us, we will not hide from their children... telling the praises of the Lord... so that the last generation may know."

"A watcher and a holy one came down from heaven" (a holy angel came down from heaven, in the words of the Book of Daniel). Such was Rabbi Israel Baal Shem Tov, the light of the world, the holy lamp, together with his companions. He was a spark of the soul of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai. So I heard from the son of Rabbi Yaakov of Mezhibuzh, who was called Rabbi Yakil: once his father took him to the study house, and the Baal Shem Tov was standing and praying before the ark. His father said to him: "My son, look and see, for a thing like this will not be seen in the world until the Messiah comes. This is Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai and his companions."

And one sees it also from the story told later in the book, with his brother-in-law Rabbi Gershon of Kitov: during the afternoon prayer the soul of the Baal Shem Tov would rise on high in order to raise up many souls. In the book *Etz Chaim* (in the Gate of the Sabbath, chapter 8) it is written that Moses our teacher was quick to do this every Sabbath eve: to raise up all the souls, of the living and of the dead. From this it appears that the Baal Shem Tov also had something of the soul of Moses our teacher. See the story itself; and what is known needs no proof.

**Summary:** The author explains that he wrote the book because miracles are growing fewer and faith is growing weaker. He writes that he wrote down only what he heard from truthful men, without adding or taking away, and that he left it to each reader to learn the moral lesson for himself. At the end he says that the Baal Shem Tov was a spark of the soul of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, and that he also had something of the soul of Moses our teacher.

## 1 — The Baal Shem Tov's Father, and the Boy Who Was Not Afraid

The printer says: In the manuscripts I copied from the author, the story of the Baal Shem Tov's family and of how he became known was not written in order. But I heard all of it in the name of our master (the Rebbe, the Rav), in the right order and with a good explanation. So I will print it as they heard it from his holy mouth, and what branches out from it I will write afterward, as it is written in the manuscripts.

The Baal Shem Tov's father, Rabbi Eliezer, lived in the land of Wallachia, near the border. He and his wife were old. Once robbers came to the town and took him captive. His wife fled to another town, and because of her great poverty she became a midwife, and that is how she made her living. The captors took Rabbi Eliezer to a distant land, where there were no Jews, and sold him there.

Rabbi Eliezer served his master faithfully. The master loved him and put him in charge of his whole household. Rabbi Eliezer asked his master for permission to rest on the Sabbath, and the master agreed. After a long time Rabbi Eliezer wanted to escape and save himself, but in a dream he was told: "Do not hurry. You still need to be in this land."

One day the master had business with the viceroy (the king's chief adviser), and he gave him Rabbi Eliezer as a gift, and praised him highly. The viceroy too loved him at once, and gave him a room of his own. Rabbi Eliezer did not have to work at all, except for one thing: when the viceroy came back from the king, Rabbi Eliezer went out to meet him with a basin of water to wash his feet, as was the custom of great noblemen. All the rest of the time he sat in his room and occupied himself with Torah and prayer.

Once the king had a great war. He called his adviser so that they could plan together how to fight, but the matter was difficult, and they could not work out what to do. The king was very angry at the viceroy, who did not know how to help in a time of trouble. The viceroy went home angry. When he arrived, Rabbi Eliezer came out to meet him with the basin of water as usual, but the viceroy refused it, and lay down on his bed with an angry face. Rabbi Eliezer said to him: "My lord, why are you angry? Tell me." And the viceroy scolded him.

But Rabbi Eliezer was a faithful servant and wanted his master's good, so he risked himself and came to him again and again, until the viceroy was forced to tell him what had happened. Rabbi Eliezer said to him: "Interpretations belong to God, and the Lord is a man of war. I will fast for several days in a row, and I will ask God about this secret, for He reveals secrets." He asked a "dream question" (he asked Heaven to answer him in a dream), and they answered him exactly how the war should be fought, all of it in detail and clearly.

The next day he came to his master and told him all the advice he had received from Heaven. The viceroy was overjoyed. He went at once to the king in great joy and said to him: "My lord the king, this is the advice." And he told him everything. When the king heard his words, he said: "This is wonderful advice. This is not human intelligence. It comes either from a man of God, on whom a higher spirit rests, or the opposite, from the spirit of impurity. And I know you, and I know that you are certainly not a man of God. If so, you are a sorcerer!"

The viceroy was forced to admit the truth and tell the king the whole story.

> (In the manuscripts the story is written like this: Once the king went out with his soldiers in ships to fight against a fortress. When they arrived, the fortress looked small to him. The king said: "It is already late in the day, and the fortress is small. We will spend the night here, a little way off, and in the morning we will not need to trouble the whole army; we will take it with a few soldiers." Rabbi Eliezer was then a sailor on one of the ships. In a dream he was told to go to the king and tell him not to make light of this battle, or else he and his whole army would be lost: in the sea, all around the city, there are iron pillars, and the city cannot be reached by ship, because the ships would overturn at once. It can be reached only through the city's entrance. And in the dream they also revealed to him the right way, with clear signs, so that they would not go wrong.
>
> In the morning the king rose early with the whole camp, and they began to wake the sailors to sail the ships. Rabbi Eliezer refused to sail, and said: "I have a secret matter to tell the king." At once they shaved him, changed his clothes, and brought him to the king in a small boat. He told the king what had been revealed to him from Heaven, and said: "If the king does not believe it, let him send a small boat with men who have been sentenced to death, and we will see what happens to them." The king did so. When the boat reached the place of the pillars, it overturned, and all the men in it were lost. The king said: "If so, give advice: what should we do?" Rabbi Eliezer said to him: "There is a straight way, the city's entrance, where the people of the city go in and out." And he showed him the signs. They did so, and the king succeeded and captured the fortress. Here ends the account in the manuscripts.)

The king made Rabbi Eliezer commander of his army, for he saw with open eyes that God was with him and that everything he did succeeded. And wherever the king sent him to fight, he was very successful. Meanwhile Rabbi Eliezer thought: "What will happen in the end? Perhaps now is the time to escape home." And again it was revealed to him from Heaven: "You still need to be in this land."

One day the viceroy died, and the king appointed Rabbi Eliezer in his place to be his adviser, because he loved him. He also gave him the viceroy's daughter as his wife. But Rabbi Eliezer, with God's help, did not come close to her as a husband to his wife. He looked for all kinds of excuses not to be at home, and even when he was at home, he did not come near her at all. In that land a Jew was forbidden to live, and a Jew who was caught there was sentenced to death. So several years passed. Once his wife said to him: "Tell me, what fault have you found in me, that you do not come near me and do not behave with me as every husband does with his wife?" He said to her: "Swear to me that you will not tell anyone, and I will tell you the truth." She swore, and he said to her: "I am a Jew." At once she sent him back to his own country, with a great deal of money and gold.

But on the way robbers attacked him and took everything he had. On the way Elijah the Prophet appeared to him and said: "Because of what you did, a son will be born to you who will light up the eyes of Israel, and the verse 'Israel, in whom I will be glorified' will come true in him." Rabbi Eliezer reached home and found his wife, thank God, alive. And in their old age the Baal Shem Tov was born to them, when both of them were close to a hundred years old. (The Baal Shem Tov said that his soul could not be brought down into the world until desire had come completely to an end.)

The child grew and was weaned. When the time came for his father to die, he took the child in his arms and said: "I see that you will light my lamp, and I have not merited to raise you. But my beloved son, remember one thing all your days: God is with you. And so do not be afraid of anything." (In the name of our master I heard a natural explanation of why these words were engraved in his heart: our Sages said that what a child says, he heard from his father or his mother, and especially a "child of old age," born to his parents when they were old. Jacob loved Joseph because he was the child of his old age, and so the bond between them was very great, as is written in the holy Zohar. And so it was here too: because of the great and true bond between them, the words were engraved in the heart of the Baal Shem Tov, even though he was still a very small child.)

After his father passed away, the child grew up. The people of the town had greatly respected his father, so they were kind to him and sent him to study with a teacher. He did very well in his studies, but he had a habit: he would study for a few days and then run away from the cheder. They searched for him and found him sitting alone in the forest. They thought this was because he was an orphan with no one to watch over him, and so he was a wild boy, and they brought him back to the teacher. This happened several times: he ran away to the forest to be alone there.

Little by little the matter was forgotten, and they gave up sending him to the teacher. The boy grew up unlike the other children. He was hired as a *belfer*, the teacher's helper: he brought the little children to cheder, and took them to the synagogue to answer "Amen, may His great name be blessed," the Kedushah and "Amen" in a sweet voice. This was his holy service, with the schoolchildren, whose breath is pure of sin. All along the way he sang with them in a sweet voice and with great eagerness, and he could be heard from far away. His service rose very high and gave great delight in Heaven, like the song the Levites sang in the Holy Temple. It was a time of great favor in Heaven.

But the Satan came among them too, for he understood how far these things reached, and he feared for himself that the time would come when he would vanish from the world. The Satan entered into a certain sorcerer. When the Baal Shem Tov was walking with the children, singing with eagerness and in a sweet voice, the sorcerer turned into a wild beast called a *vukilak* (a kind of werewolf), and attacked them. They all fled in fear, and some of the children even fell ill. And so this regular service came to a stop.

After a while the Baal Shem Tov remembered his father's words, not to be afraid of anything because God was with him, and he grew strong in his faith in God. He went to the householders, the children's fathers, and spoke to their hearts so that they would bring the children back to him: "I will stand against the beast and kill it in God's name. Why should the holy service of the children stop because of it, when it is such a great thing?" And they listened to him. He took a good, strong stick in his hand. When he was walking with the children in song and joy, the beast attacked them. He ran at it, struck it on the forehead, and it died. The next day they found the gentile sorcerer lying dead on the ground.

From then on he became the watchman of the study house. And this was his custom: as long as the scholars in the study house were awake, he slept. When they went to sleep, he woke up and did his service in full, until the time came when people wake up, and then he went back to sleep. Everyone thought he slept the whole night, from beginning to end.

**Summary:** Rabbi Eliezer, the Baal Shem Tov's father, was taken captive to a distant land, rose to greatness with the king through advice he received from Heaven, and kept his Judaism until he was set free. Elijah the Prophet promised him a son, and the Baal Shem Tov was born when both his parents were nearly a hundred. Before his death his father told him: "God is with you; do not be afraid of anything." When a sorcerer turned into a wild beast and frightened the children, the boy remembered his father's words and killed the beast, and afterward he became the watchman of the study house and served God in secret at night.

## 2 — The Writings of Rabbi Adam, and the Secret Wedding

> (I heard from Rabbi Shimshon, the rabbi of Rashkov, son of the Rabbi of Polonne, a story about a man named Rabbi Adam. It was from him that the writings reached the Baal Shem Tov: once Rabbi Adam went into a cave, and found there writings of the secrets of the Torah.
>
> Once Rabbi Adam invited the emperor to a feast, and the emperor promised to come. Rabbi Adam lived in a very small house. And the feast came about like this: he saw that one king was preparing a feast for another king. The preparations went on for two years, so that in that time great palaces could be built. They built upper stories of beautiful, splendid crystal glass, and over the stories ran a channel of water, with fish swimming back and forth in it. They prepared credenzas of silver and gold, food, drink and servants, as befits the honor of a king, and set a day when the visiting king would come to the feast. And for that very day Rabbi Adam invited the emperor. And the emperor traveled with all his ministers to Rabbi Adam, to the feast.
>
> Among the ministers was one who hated the Jews very much. He tried to persuade the emperor not to go, because he would be put to shame when it turned out that Rabbi Adam had neither a palace nor a feast. The emperor did not listen to him and went. On the way the minister again tried to persuade him to go back to the imperial court, and again the emperor did not listen. Even so, the emperor sent a minister to see whether Rabbi Adam had prepared anything for the feast, and whether there was room in his house. The messenger came back and said that he had a small house, as before. Even so, the emperor went on. When he came near the town, he sent another messenger, and he came back with the same answer. But when the emperor reached the street where Rabbi Adam lived, he saw great palaces and a great courtyard. He went into the courtyard with his ministers and servants. At once the servants took all the horses to the stables, and each servant did his job, but they were all mute. They ate and drank of all good things. Rabbi Adam said to them: "Eat and drink as much as you like, only let none of you take any vessel from here."
>
> Afterward Rabbi Adam said: "Let each one say what he wants, 'This is the thing I want,' and put his hand into the pocket of his garment, and he will find the thing there." The emperor began, said what he wanted, and found it in the pocket of his garment. And all the ministers did the same. When the turn came of the minister who hated the Jews, he too said something, and Rabbi Adam said to him: "Put your hand into the pocket of your garment." When he put his hand into the pocket, it got dirty, and he pulled it out smeared with excrement, with a great stench. The emperor ordered him thrown out. The minister washed his hand in water, and it did not help. He began to plead with Rabbi Adam. Rabbi Adam said to him: "If you take it upon yourself not to hate the Jews anymore, well and good. And if not, your hands will be dirty all the days of your life." The minister took it upon himself. Rabbi Adam said to him: "There is no remedy for you unless a Jew urinates on your hand. With that you will wash, and that will help you." And so it was. <!-- MATURE: crude bodily content (excrement on the minister's hand; cured by a Jew urinating on it) -->
>
> The emperor took two cups from one of the credenzas, and hid them in his garment. After the emperor left, the palaces vanished, and of all the things only the two cups were missing, and what they had eaten and drunk. The newspapers of the world wrote of the wonder: the palaces had vanished with all the preparations, and afterward they came back whole; only two cups were missing, and what had been eaten and drunk. When the newspapers reached the emperor, he sent a letter to that king, and wrote in it: "I have a Jew here who brought us your palace, and we ate the feast you prepared. As a sign, I am sending you your two cups."
>
> Once Rabbi Adam's wife said to him: "How long will we suffer this poverty?" He said to her: "What do you want? Whatever garment you like, go to the storeroom and take a coat from the rod, whichever you want." She went to the storeroom, took a coat from the rod as she wished, and brought it home. But Rabbi Adam urged her to return it, saying: "It is better for us to suffer poverty in this world." And she did so, and returned the coat. Rabbi Adam asked a dream question: to whom should he hand over his writings? And they answered him: "Hand them over to Rabbi Israel son of Eliezer, of the town of Okup.")

Before his death Rabbi Adam commanded his only son: "I have writings of the secrets of the Torah, but you are not worthy of them. Look for a town called Okup. There you will find a man named Israel son of Eliezer, who is about fourteen years old. Hand the writings over to him, for they belong to the root of his soul. And if you merit that he will study with you, how good that will be."

After Rabbi Adam passed away, his only son, who was very great in Torah and perfect in every virtue, took a wagon and horses and traveled from town to town until he came to Okup. He stayed with the *parnas* of the month (the man who ran the community that month). The parnas asked him: "Where are you from and where are you going? For by the look of it, you have not come to collect money." He answered him: "My late father was a famous tzaddik, and before his passing he commanded me to marry here, in Okup, and I must carry out all his words." At once the whole town was astir, and many matches were offered to him, for everyone who saw him loved him. He made a match with the family of a certain rich man.

After the wedding he began to look for the man he was seeking, and found no one except Israel, the caretaker of the study house. When he looked at him closely, it seemed to him that he was not what he appeared to be from the outside, and that perhaps this was the man he was looking for. He said to his father-in-law: "Your house is noisy, and it is hard for me to study. Do me a favor: build me a small room in the study house, so that I can study there in peace. As our Sages said: 'Where there is song, there let there be prayer.'" His intention was to be near Israel all the time, so as to get to know him deeply. The father-in-law did it without delay, for his son-in-law was very dear to him. He also ordered the Baal Shem Tov to serve his son-in-law, and paid him for it.

One night, when everyone was asleep, the rabbi's son pretended that he too was asleep. The Baal Shem Tov rose as was his custom to study Torah and serve God, and the rabbi's son watched him once and again. On the third night the Baal Shem Tov rose and studied, and in the middle of it he fell asleep. The rabbi's son got up, took one booklet of the writings, laid it before him, and again pretended to be asleep. When the Baal Shem Tov woke and saw the booklet, he trembled greatly, studied it, and hid it in his clothes. The rabbi's son did the same on another night too, until he saw clearly that this was the man to whom his father had commanded him to hand over the writings. He called him and said to him: "My father left me these writings, and commanded me to hand them over to you. Here are all the writings before you. But I ask a great favor of you: that you study with me." The Baal Shem Tov answered him: "Good, but on condition that no one knows of it except you, and that you show no sign of it. On the contrary, go on ordering me to serve you as before." And so they did.

Afterward the rabbi's son said to his father-in-law: "Now I want more seclusion. If possible, get me a house of my own outside the town, and I will sit there over Torah and the service of God." And the father-in-law did so. He asked his father-in-law to hire the boy Israel to live with him, to keep him company, and to serve him whenever he needed to send him somewhere. And the father-in-law did so, for his son-in-law was greatly respected. When the people of the community saw that Israel was studying with the rabbi's son, they said: "Surely his father's merit caused the rabbi's son to come here and draw him close," for it seemed to them that now he was going in a good way. They found him a wife, but after a short time his wife died.

In the house of seclusion they studied everything that was needed: Gemara, Rashi, Tosafot, the codes of law, and also all the holy writings. In the writings there was divine Kabbalah, and also "practical Kabbalah" (using holy names to act in the world). Once the rabbi's son asked the Baal Shem Tov to bring down the "Prince of the Torah" (the angel of the Torah), so that he would explain something to him. The Baal Shem Tov put him off, saying: "If we make a mistake in the mystical intention, there may be danger, for we have no ashes of the red heifer (with which one is purified)." The rabbi's son urged him day after day, until he could not refuse. They fasted from Sabbath to Sabbath without a break, immersed in the mikveh properly, and at the end of the Sabbath they concentrated on the known mystical intentions. At once the Baal Shem Tov cried out: "Oh, oh, we made a mistake! The Prince of Fire has come down, and he will surely burn the whole town! So you, whom everyone holds to be a very pious man, go quickly to your father-in-law and to the whole town, so that they save themselves, for the town will soon burn." And so it was.

From then on everyone held the rabbi's son to be a man of God and a wonder-worker. After a long time he again urged the Baal Shem Tov to bring down the Prince of the Torah. The Baal Shem Tov refused, but the rabbi's son urged him for several days, until he was forced to do what he wanted. They fasted as the time before, and at the end of the Sabbath, when they concentrated on the known mystical intentions, the Baal Shem Tov cried out: "Alas! Because of this, death has been decreed on both of us tonight. But we have a remedy: if we stay strong and stand all night and concentrate on the mystical intentions, without tasting the least sleep, the judgment will be canceled. But if one of us dozes off, then, since sleep comes from the side of death, the accuser will get a hold to rule over him completely." So they did all night. Before daybreak the rabbi's son could not hold out, and dozed off a little. When the Baal Shem Tov saw this, he ran quickly and made a great noise. The rabbi's son suddenly fainted away. They tried to wake him, and it did not help. They buried him with great honor.

Afterward the Baal Shem Tov took another path. He left the place, went to the community near Brody, and became a teacher there. Everyone loved him, for he was a very great scholar and very wise, so that all the affairs of the community were decided by him. When people had a quarrel and came to him, he brought the law to light, and both sides, the one in the right and the one in the wrong, were pleased with him, because in his great wisdom he spoke to their hearts so that it would seem good to everyone.

Rabbi Gershon of Kitov was the head of the rabbinical court in Brody. His father, Rabbi Avraham, had a lawsuit with a man of the community where the Baal Shem Tov was. Rabbi Avraham came there and told his opponent to travel with him to Brody, to settle the dispute by Torah law. The man said to him: "We have a teacher here who is great in Torah, and he is a righteous judge. Every time a case comes before him, both sides are pleased, because he explains the ruling well. Let us go to him and put our claims before him, and if you are not satisfied, I will travel with you to Brody." Rabbi Avraham agreed, and they came before him.

The moment Rabbi Avraham came before the Baal Shem Tov, the Baal Shem Tov saw with the holy spirit that Rabbi Avraham's daughter was his destined match. In those days it was the custom of great scholars, when an important guest came to them, to say before him an explanation of the Torah. The Baal Shem Tov said before him a very sharp explanation of Maimonides, and several more explanations, until Rabbi Avraham's soul was drawn after him and bound to his soul. Afterward the two sides presented their claims. There were many claims and quarrels between them, and within about an hour the Baal Shem Tov brought out the true judgment. Rabbi Avraham was greatly amazed, and loved him greatly.

Meanwhile Rabbi Avraham learned that the Baal Shem Tov was unmarried and needed to marry. Rabbi Avraham had a divorced daughter. He came to him in secret and said to him: "I have heard that you need a wife. Perhaps you will agree to marry my daughter?" The Baal Shem Tov answered him: "Good. But in this community many rich men want me as a son-in-law, so the matter must be kept secret, so that no one knows. I could not face them, after they have always been good to me and honored me. If you agree, we will write the engagement contract in secret. And I ask one more thing of you: make the match with me, not with my Torah and my wisdom. I do not want you to write any praises or virtues about me at all, only 'Israel son of Eliezer.'" Because Rabbi Avraham's soul was bound to his soul, he agreed to everything he said. They wrote the engagement contract without titles, and without writing the name of the place, and no one knew of it.

On the way home Rabbi Avraham fell ill with the illness from which he died, and he passed away. They sent word to his son, the great Rabbi Gershon of Kitov, in Brody, and he came to eulogize his father as is proper. He received all the papers that were found among his father's things, and among them he found the engagement contract: his father had matched his daughter, Rabbi Gershon's sister, with a man named Israel. Rabbi Gershon was very surprised: his father was a famous man; how had he made a match with a simple man, and with a "so-and-so" at that, of whom no one knew where he was from or what family he came from? He told his sister about it. She said to him: "If our father saw him as suitable, we have nothing to doubt."

The Baal Shem Tov waited until his term of work as a teacher was over. He came to the householder and said: "I am going back to my place." They spoke to his heart and offered him a higher salary to stay with them, but he did not agree, for that was not his intention. He disguised himself: he changed his clothes to look like one of the good-for-nothings, in a short fur coat and a wide belt, changed his manners and his speech, and went to Brody to the house of Rabbi Gershon. Two judges sat before Rabbi Gershon, and he stood beside them, looking all the time into the cases that came before them. The Baal Shem Tov stood by the door. Rabbi Gershon thought he was a poor man, took a coin and wanted to give it to him. The Baal Shem Tov said to him: "I have a secret matter to tell you." He went with him into a room, showed him the engagement contract, and said to him: "Give me my wife." When Rabbi Gershon saw the man, his clothes and his speech, he was very frightened and astonished: what had his father done? He called his sister and told her everything. She answered him: "If our father did this, we must not doubt him. Surely it comes from God, and perhaps good offspring will come from him." And so they did, and set the time of the wedding.

Before the wedding canopy, the Baal Shem Tov said that he wanted first to speak with his wife, as our Sages said. He spoke with her in secret, and revealed the truth to her, all of it. He made her swear not to reveal anything of it, even if many times of great poverty should pass over her.

After the wedding Rabbi Gershon wanted to study with him; perhaps he would take in a little Torah. But the Baal Shem Tov hid himself, as if he were unable to take in anything at all. Rabbi Gershon said to his sister: "The truth is that your husband is a great disgrace to me. If you want to divorce him, well and good. And if not, I will buy you a horse, and you will go with him to live wherever you want, for I cannot bear this disgrace." She agreed. They set out, and the Baal Shem Tov chose a place for her to live, and he himself went to seclude himself among great mountains. And this was her livelihood: two or three times a week she came to him with a horse and wagon, he dug clay, and she carted it to the town, and from this she made her living.

The Baal Shem Tov always fasted long fasts. When he wanted to eat, he dug a hollow in the ground, put flour and water in it, and the heat of the sun baked it. That was all his food after a fast, and all his days he lived in seclusion. The mountains were very high, and between them was a deep valley, as steep as a smooth wall. Once the Baal Shem Tov was deep in contemplation, and walked on the mountains. On the other mountain stood robbers. They saw him from afar walking to the edge of the mountain, lost in thought, and said: "He will surely fall into the valley and be dashed to pieces." But as he came near the valley, the other mountain came near to him and became level ground, and he crossed over; and afterward the two mountains moved apart as before. And when he came back, as he came near the valley, the first mountain came near the second and became level ground. And so it happened several times, going and coming back.

The robbers saw that he was a man of God, that God was with him. They made peace with him, came before him and told him all they had seen, and said: "We have seen with our own eyes that you are a man of God. So we ask you to pray for us, that God make our way succeed, for we always go on it at the risk of our lives." The Baal Shem Tov said to them: "If you swear to me that you will not harm a Jew or rob him, I will do as you ask." They swore to it. From then on, when they had a quarrel or a claim among themselves, they came before him, and he made a compromise between them.

Once two robbers came before him for judgment, and one of them was not satisfied with the ruling. He said in his heart: "When the Baal Shem Tov is awake, no harm can be done to him. But when he is asleep, I will do as I wish and kill him." And so he did: when the Baal Shem Tov was asleep, the robber came and raised his axe to cut off his head. At once he was seized, he did not know by whom, and beaten with cruel blows, until there was hardly any life left in him and he had no strength to speak. The Baal Shem Tov woke, and saw before him the beaten man, covered with blood and wounds. He asked him: "Who are you, and who did this to you?" And he had no strength to answer. And the Baal Shem Tov went on his way. The next day, when he had recovered a little, the robber told him everything that had happened.

**Summary:** Rabbi Adam, a wonder-worker who found writings of the secrets of the Torah in a cave, commanded his son to hand them over to the boy Israel son of Eliezer of Okup. The son found the Baal Shem Tov, who was hiding as a simple caretaker, and studied with him, but he died after he urged him to bring down the "Prince of the Torah" and could not stay awake all night. Afterward Rabbi Avraham secretly matched his daughter with the Baal Shem Tov, and her brother, Rabbi Gershon, thought he was a simple man and sent the couple away from the town. The Baal Shem Tov secluded himself among the mountains, and robbers who saw a miracle done for him swore not to harm Jews.

## 3 — The Frog in the Wilderness

(Once the robbers came to the Baal Shem Tov and said: "Our master, we know a short way to the Land of Israel, through caves and tunnels. If you like, come with us, and we will show you the way." And he agreed to go with them. On the way there was a deep valley, full of water, mud and mire. They crossed it on a plank that lay from one side to the other, leaning on a stake they had driven into the swamp. The robbers crossed first. When the Baal Shem Tov wanted to cross, he saw that here was "the flame of the ever-turning sword" (like the sword that guarded the way to the Tree of Life in the Garden of Eden), and he turned back, because crossing there was a great danger for him. The Baal Shem Tov said in his heart: "Surely I did not come here for nothing." And on the way back he met the frog that is told about here. And some tell it like this:)

Once the Baal Shem Tov went into a very deep contemplation, and walked like that for three days and three nights without knowing what was happening around him. Afterward he realized that he was in a great wilderness, very far from his home. He wondered greatly how he had come to such a wilderness, and thought: "Surely this is not for nothing." Meanwhile a very large frog came before him, so large that he could hardly tell what kind of creature it was. He asked it: "Who are you?" And the frog said that it was a Torah scholar who had been reincarnated in this frog. (The Baal Shem Tov said to him: "A Torah scholar you are!" and with these words he raised him up a great deal.) The frog told him that he had already been reincarnated in it for five hundred years.

Although the holy Arizal had repaired all the souls, this soul, because of its great sins, had been sent far away to a place where there are no people, so that it would not be repaired. The Baal Shem Tov asked him what his sin had been. He told him that once he had made light of washing his hands, and did not wash his hands properly. The Satan accused him, and Heaven answered the Satan: "He cannot be condemned for one transgression. But one transgression drags another after it: if you manage to make him stumble in another transgression, we will count the first one against him too. And if he remembers God and repents, he will be clean of it as well." The Satan tempted him with another transgression, and he stumbled in it and did not withstand the test. And so it went, again and again, until he had transgressed almost the whole Torah.

The ruling in Heaven was to push him away and not accept his repentance. Even so, had he pressed at the gates of repentance, they would have accepted him, as is known from the story of "Acher" (Elisha ben Abuyah), about whom a heavenly voice went out: "Return, wayward children, except for Acher." That was the punishment for the sin: to push him away. But had he pressed and repented, they would have accepted him, for nothing stands in the way of repentance. But the Satan led him astray, and he became a great drunkard, until he had no time to reflect and repent, and he committed every transgression in the world.

And since the cause of all the sins was the first sin, making light of washing the hands, when he died he was reincarnated in a frog, which is always in water, and in a place where there are no people. For when a Jew passes by there, or says a blessing, or thinks a good thought, he can bring out the precious from the worthless. And the Baal Shem Tov made a *tikkun* for his soul and raised it up, until the frog was left dead.

**Summary:** The Baal Shem Tov walked for three days lost in thought, found himself in a distant wilderness, and there met a giant frog. For five hundred years the soul of a Torah scholar had been reincarnated in the frog; once he had made light of washing his hands, and from there he sank until he had committed every transgression. The Baal Shem Tov repaired his soul and raised it up, and the frog died.

## 4 — Tefillin on the Intermediate Days, and the Unfit Mezuzah

Afterward Rabbi Gershon leased a village for the Baal Shem Tov, so that he could make a living from it, and there the Baal Shem Tov reached great perfection. He built himself a house of seclusion in the forest, and there he prayed and studied all day and all night, the whole week. Only from Sabbath to Sabbath did he come home, and there he also had white clothes for the Sabbath. He also had a bathhouse and a mikveh. His wife saw to their livelihood, and God sent blessing and success in the work of her hands. They took in guests, and gave them food and drink with great honor. When a guest came, she sent for her husband, and he came and served them, and they knew nothing about him. And his custom was that when he came to town for Rosh Hashanah, he stayed in town the whole month.

Once, on the intermediate days of Sukkot, Rabbi Gershon saw that the Baal Shem Tov was not putting on tefillin. (The Baal Shem Tov used to pray precisely by the eastern wall.) Rabbi Gershon asked him: "Why are you not putting on tefillin today?" He answered him: "I saw in books in Yiddish that whoever puts on tefillin on the intermediate days of a festival deserves death." Rabbi Gershon was very angry, because the Baal Shem Tov was deciding Jewish law from books in Yiddish, and who knew what else would come of it.

Rabbi Gershon took him to the rabbi of the community, so that he would give him words of moral reproof. (For they held the Baal Shem Tov to be God-fearing, only "an ignorant man cannot fear sin": someone who has not studied does not know how to guard himself against sin.) The rabbi was a very great tzaddik. When they reached the rabbi's house, Rabbi Gershon kissed the mezuzah, but the Baal Shem Tov only put his hand on the mezuzah and did not kiss it. Rabbi Gershon was angry with him for this too. When they entered the rabbi's house, the Baal Shem Tov removed his "veil" (his concealment), and the rabbi saw a great light and stood up before him. Then the Baal Shem Tov put the veil back, and the rabbi sat down. And he did this several times. The rabbi was very frightened, for he did not understand what this was: at one moment the man looked to him like a holy man, and at another like a simple man.

When Rabbi Gershon complained to the rabbi about the tefillin and the mezuzah, the rabbi took the Baal Shem Tov aside and said to him: "I decree that you reveal the truth to me." The Baal Shem Tov was forced to reveal it to him, but in return he decreed that the rabbi too would reveal nothing of it. When they went outside, the rabbi said to Rabbi Gershon: "I have reproved him. In my opinion he will do nothing improper, for he acts in simple innocence." And the rabbi went and took the mezuzah, and they examined it, and it turned out to be unfit.

**Summary:** Rabbi Gershon saw that the Baal Shem Tov did not put on tefillin on the intermediate days of the festival and did not kiss the mezuzah, and took him to the town rabbi so that he would reprove him. The Baal Shem Tov showed the rabbi his light for a moment, and afterward revealed the truth to him in secret. The rabbi told Rabbi Gershon that the Baal Shem Tov acted in simple innocence, and they examined the mezuzah and found that it was unfit.

## 5 — The Scribe, the Madwoman, and the Age of Thirty-Six

First of all, I heard from my father-in-law, Rabbi Alexander the shochet. He was the scribe of the holy tzaddik, the teacher of all the Jews in exile, Rabbi Israel Baal Shem Tov of Mezhibuzh, for eight years, some years before the famous pious scribe Rabbi Tzvi came. Afterward they were both scribes, because the work was too much for one. Afterward my father-in-law became a shochet and meat inspector, because he had sorrow in raising his children. The Baal Shem Tov gave him a post in Sde Lavan, and afterward he was a shochet in Berditchev. And in his old age the Baal Shem Tov gave my father-in-law a letter to the great rabbi of Nemirov, the author of the book *Toldot Yaakov Yosef*, and my father-in-law became the shochet and meat inspector in Nemirov.

Several times I heard this rabbi say to my father-in-law, as words of moral teaching at the third Sabbath meal: "Remember what the Baal Shem Tov said to you: 'Once I went to the Garden of Eden, and a great many Jews went with me. And the closer I came to the Garden of Eden, the fewer people there were, until when I reached the Tree of Life only a few were left with me. Among them I also saw Senderl, and you stayed a little behind.'"

My father-in-law did not have whole fingernails on his right hand: on each finger two nails grew, one at the tip of the finger and one at its base, and between the two nails there was flesh that separated them. When the upper ones grew, he cut them every Sabbath eve, until the upper nail was used up. Meanwhile the lower one rose to the tip of the finger and came away from the base, and another began to grow. And where did this come from? Once the Baal Shem Tov sent him to adjure a madman (to drive a spirit out of him). At night, in his sleep, the spirit frightened him. In his mind he drew holy names on each and every finger of his hand, like a drawing of a hand he had seen in books, and slapped the spirit on the cheek, and struck the wall until blood came out from under all his nails, and all the nails fell off. From that day on his nails grew like that. And at once the Baal Shem Tov came and said to him (so my father-in-law told): "Were you frightened? Do not worry and do not be afraid."

And now I will tell the great things I heard from my father-in-law, how the Baal Shem Tov became known. He lived in a small village and kept a tavern. And his custom was this: after he had brought his wife the vodka, he went off to seclude himself across the river Prut. There, on the mountain, he had a rock that looked like a small house. He took with him one loaf of bread for one meal, and ate once a week. So he fasted for several years, and on the Sabbath eve he came back home. His brother-in-law, Rabbi Gershon of Kitov, held him to be an ignorant boor, and tried to persuade his sister to divorce him. But she refused, for she knew who he was, but she did not reveal it to anyone.

Once the Baal Shem Tov came to town. There was a madwoman there who revealed to everyone his bad deeds and his good deeds. Rabbi Gershon asked the pious rabbi, the head of the rabbinical court of Kitov, Rabbi Moshe, to bring the Baal Shem Tov to the woman; perhaps he would hear moral reproof from her and mend his ways. They all went. When the rabbi of Kitov came in, the woman said to him: "Welcome, holy and pure one," and so to each one according to his rank. The Baal Shem Tov came in last. When she saw him, she said: "Welcome, Rabbi Israel" (for he was still young then). And she said to him: "Do you think I am afraid of you? No! For I know that you were warned from Heaven not to use the holy names until you reach the age of thirty-six." And he tried to hide and not draw attention to himself. The people asked her: "What are you saying?" And she repeated her words before them, until the Baal Shem Tov rebuked her and said: "Will you not be quiet? I will set up a rabbinical court over this, and they will permit me, and I will drive you out of this woman!" She began to plead: "I will be quiet!"

The Hasidim who had come with him urged him to ask permission and drive the spirit out of the woman. The Baal Shem Tov asked them not to do it, for it is very dangerous, but they would not let him be. Then the Baal Shem Tov said to the spirit: "See what you have caused. So my advice is: leave the woman of your own will, and we will all study for you." He asked the spirit its name, and the spirit said: "I cannot reveal it before all the people. Let the people leave, and I will reveal it to you, for I have sons in this town." When the people left, he revealed to him who he was. My father-in-law knew him too. And he had become a spirit only because he had mocked the Hasidim of Kitov. The spirit left the woman of its own will. And from then on they did not let the Baal Shem Tov live in that village, and a tollkeeper took him on as a teacher for his sons.

My father-in-law also told me: the tollkeeper who took him on as a teacher told him that he had only one house, and that it was held to be an unclean house. The Baal Shem Tov said he would live in it, and set the attic apart for the "outside ones" (the harmful spirits). And when they made mischief, he rebuked them, and they fell silent.

There was another story: rain was badly needed, and the gentiles brought out their idols and went around the village with them, as was their custom, and no rain fell. The Baal Shem Tov said to the tollkeeper: "Send word to the whole area to come here for a minyan." He decreed a fast, and led the prayers before the ark himself, and they prayed at great length. A gentile asked: "Why did you pray so long today, with great weeping among you?" The tollkeeper told him the truth, that they had prayed for rain. The gentile mocked greatly and said: "They went around with their idols and it did no good, and what good will you do with your prayer?" The tollkeeper told the Baal Shem Tov what the gentile had said, and the Baal Shem Tov said to him: "Go and tell the gentile that today there will be rain." And so it was.

Afterward he settled in the town of Tluste, and there too he was a teacher. He had no way to make a minyan in his house, so he gathered people and prayed with them. He wore only a plain coarse garment, and his toes stuck out through the holes in his shoes, for he was very poor. Before prayer he went to the mikveh, even in the cold month of Tevet, and during prayer drops of sweat as big as beans ran down him. Afterward some sick people came to him, and he did not want to receive them. Until once they brought him a madman or a madwoman, and he did not want to receive that one either. At night he was told that he had reached thirty-six years of age. In the morning he calculated, and saw that it was true. He received the madman and healed him, left his work as a teacher, and took my father-in-law to be his scribe. And from then on people traveled to him from everywhere.

**Summary:** Rabbi Alexander, the author's father-in-law, was the Baal Shem Tov's scribe, and told of the strange nails that grew on his hand after he fought an evil spirit. The Baal Shem Tov secluded himself on a mountain for years and was taken for an ignoramus, until a woman with a spirit in her revealed that he was forbidden to use holy names before the age of thirty-six, and he persuaded the spirit to leave her. Afterward he was a teacher, and his prayer brought rain. When he reached thirty-six he healed a madman, left his work as a teacher, and people began to travel to him from everywhere.

## 6 — The Witchcraft in the Tollkeeper's House

Once a tollkeeper from a small town came to the Baal Shem Tov; witchcraft had been done to him: of everything that was in his house, in every vessel, and even of the money in his pocket, only half was left after the night had passed over it. The Baal Shem Tov ordered the scribe to write protective amulets for all of the tollkeeper's rooms, and traveled with him. When they reached his house, the Baal Shem Tov put the amulets in a pile on the table, and went to the market. The people of the house saw the spirit begin to roll like a whirlwind from corner to corner, until it broke away from the house and left it.

Around the house there was a large courtyard, and in it a small gate. When the Baal Shem Tov came back to his lodging, it seemed to him that by the small gate stood something like a very big Cossack. And the closer the Baal Shem Tov came to the courtyard, the smaller the *kelipah* (the force of impurity) became, until when he came near, it vanished. The Baal Shem Tov set amulets there, and the people of the house told him how the spirit had rolled and left the house. The Baal Shem Tov also turned the bed over and searched the rooms, among the barrels that stood there, to drive out the kelipah, for that was his custom. And then he traveled home.

At once the miller's wife, a gentile, came weeping, and said to the tollkeeper in Russian: "Ya mudra, ale ty mudrishe" (probably: "I am clever, but you are cleverer"), for the spirit had gone to her house in anger, and she had not been at home, and the spirit had killed two creeping creatures.

**Summary:** A tollkeeper whose house had been bewitched, so that every night half of everything he had disappeared, asked the Baal Shem Tov for help. The Baal Shem Tov came to his house with amulets, and the evil spirit rolled out of the house, and the figure of a giant Cossack that stood at the gate grew smaller and smaller until it vanished. Right afterward the gentile miller's wife came weeping, and said that the spirit had come to her house in anger.

## 7 — The Tollkeeper Behind Whom the Angel of Death Was Dancing

Another time a tollkeeper came to the Baal Shem Tov whose luck had gone very bad. The Baal Shem Tov saw that the Angel of Death was dancing behind him, and spoke harsh words to him, so that he would repent: "Why chase after the possessions of this world? Better that you repent and mend your ways." The tollkeeper thought the Baal Shem Tov wanted money, and wanted to give him money, but the Baal Shem Tov did not accept it. When the tollkeeper left, the Baal Shem Tov said: "What a fool! Today or tomorrow he will die, and he worries about his luck." His brother-in-law, Rabbi Gershon, rebuked him and said: "Why do you say evil things about a Jew?" He answered him: "What can I do for you, if you are blind and did not see that the Angel of Death is walking behind him?" And the tollkeeper went home.

At that time another brother-in-law of the Baal Shem Tov, Rabbi Yaakov, was a widower, and he traveled to get married. His way passed by the tollkeeper's place, for they had the same road. When he stopped for the night, he stayed in a village near the tollkeeper's village, and the tollkeeper made him a proper feast. Rabbi Yaakov did not want to eat meat, and they set a dairy dish for him at the end of the table, and the tollkeeper mocked. Afterward they went to sleep. In the night the tollkeeper woke with a great headache and a great cry, and asked that someone say the confession with him. There was no one there to say the confession with him, and Rabbi Yaakov said the confession with him, and the tollkeeper repeated the confession after him in a chant. Rabbi Yaakov wondered at this. Afterward the tollkeeper's wife left the room, for she said: "Perhaps he will sleep a little, and feel better." A short time later she looked at him, and he was dead. Rabbi Yaakov went back home and declared: "Truly we are blind."

**Summary:** The Baal Shem Tov saw the Angel of Death dancing behind a tollkeeper, and warned him to repent, but the tollkeeper thought he wanted money, and Rabbi Gershon was angry with the Baal Shem Tov for speaking ill of a Jew. A short time later Rabbi Yaakov, another brother-in-law of the Baal Shem Tov, stayed with the same tollkeeper, and the tollkeeper died that night, after saying the confession. Rabbi Yaakov came back and admitted: "Truly we are blind."

## 8 — The Rabbis of Krivush and Sde Lavan

It once happened that the Baal Shem Tov was traveling and came to the town of Krivush. The head of the rabbinical court in the town was the pious Rabbi Abba, who later was head of the rabbinical court in Sde Lavan. A relative by marriage was staying with Rabbi Abba, and they were debating a passage of the Mishnah. When the Baal Shem Tov came, they did not go out to meet him. He came in and said: "God be with you! What were you studying?" And they showed him a different Mishnah. He said to them: "I know that you were studying a different Mishnah. But this Mishnah, how do you learn it?" And he showed them a great difficulty in that Mishnah. Afterward he told them its explanation, and left them, and about a parsah away (a few kilometers) he stopped to let the horses graze in a field. Rabbi Abba and his relative began to say to each other: "How did we learn that Mishnah?!" At once they hired a wagon and chased after him, found him in the field, and stayed with him. And from then on Rabbi Abba traveled to the Baal Shem Tov literally every month, for one Sabbath. Once he spent the Sabbath with him, and in the evening prayer he made a mistake and began to say "Atah Chonen" (a weekday blessing). When he came home, the Baal Shem Tov said: "How beautifully the rabbi of Krivush prayed 'Atah Chonen'!"

When the Baal Shem Tov came to Sde Lavan, there was a righteous head of the rabbinical court there, named Rabbi Chaim. The Baal Shem Tov prayed in the fields, and when they reached the town, they were still praying in the synagogue. He went to hear the Kedushah, and stood in the vestibule of the synagogue. He ordered the gabbai to go and pull off the wall the "menorah" (a tablet with a picture of a menorah and verses) that hung opposite the rabbi. The gabbai did so. When the rabbi finished his prayer, he saw that the menorah was no longer in front of him. He asked what had happened, and they told him that the Baal Shem Tov was standing in the vestibule. Right after the Kedushah he went out to him in the vestibule and asked him, and the Baal Shem Tov said to him: "You have one stray thought in your prayer because of this menorah." There are different opinions about this: some say that the scribe who wrote the menorah played with a dog while he was writing it, and some say he was a very wicked man.

The preacher of that community was the great and famous tzaddik Rabbi Avraham Podlisker. Once not a single slaughtering knife pleased the people of the community. They sent for several shochetim from several communities, and none of them managed to make them a knife that pleased them. Once the Baal Shem Tov came to them. They said: "The guest is very dear to us, but we have nothing to honor him with," and told him what had happened. He said he would make them a knife, and he made them a knife, and it pleased them. They slaughtered and ate. The Baal Shem Tov promised to send them a shochet, and he sent them my father-in-law, and he grew rich there.

My father-in-law told that the pious rabbi lived for several years while he was there, until he passed away. When the rabbi fell seriously ill, he sent for the Baal Shem Tov, and the Baal Shem Tov stayed with him a long time and worked at healing him. When the rabbi's sons saw that it had no effect, they sent for the doctor from Ostroh, and the doctor set out for Sde Lavan. The Baal Shem Tov saw that the doctor was on his way, and said to the rabbi: "I am leaving here, for the doctor from Ostroh is on his way here." The rabbi did not know about it, and begged him very much not to go. Because of the many pleas the Baal Shem Tov stayed there overnight, and said that the doctor was staying the night two parsahs from the town. The doctor had long ago raised his hand against the Baal Shem Tov, and said: "When I see the Baal Shem Tov, I will kill him with the gun in my hand." In the morning the Baal Shem Tov hurried to leave, but the sick rabbi held him back, until the doctor reached the town. The Baal Shem Tov said: "Here, the doctor has come to town." He said goodbye to the rabbi, got into the wagon and drove off. When he came opposite the doctor's lodging, he got down from the wagon, went into the house and greeted him. The doctor asked him: "Where do you know medicine from?" He answered him: "God taught me." And he went home. The sick rabbi's sons brought the doctor to their father, and to everything the doctor said to do, they said to him: "The Baal Shem Tov has already done that." And the doctor left disappointed. Then the sick rabbi complained about his sons: "What have you done? Even if the Baal Shem Tov did not heal me, when he came in to me I knew that the Shechinah came with him. And when the doctor came in to me, it seemed to me that a priest had come in to me."

After his passing the people of Sde Lavan took Rabbi Abba of Krivush as head of the rabbinical court. And in connection with him I will also write another story about the Baal Shem Tov, which I heard from one of the people of our community, the pious Rabbi Yitzchak Isaac, son of the rabbi of Brahin, who heard it from his father. The body of the story I did not want to write, for a hidden reason of my own. The rabbi of Brahin was in Sde Lavan one winter with the rabbi of Sde Lavan, and the rabbi showed him letters from the Baal Shem Tov. And these are the words of the letter, from that winter: "You will come back from the road, and you will find in the courtyard of your house a great commotion, men, women and children, and you will faint from fear. When they wake you, order at once that blood be let from two of your veins, and send at once a special messenger to tell me about the commotion. Although, thank God, I have eyes to see from afar, even so send me letters by messenger. So says Israel Baal Shem Tov." The root of the story I did not want to reveal.

**Summary:** The Baal Shem Tov showed Rabbi Abba of Krivush and his relative that they did not understand a Mishnah, and from then on Rabbi Abba traveled to him every month. In Sde Lavan the Baal Shem Tov had a "menorah" taken down from the wall that caused the rabbi a stray thought in prayer, made the community a slaughtering knife, and sent them a shochet. When the pious rabbi of the town fell ill, the Baal Shem Tov treated him, and went in to greet a doctor who had threatened to kill him, and the rabbi said that the Shechinah came in with the Baal Shem Tov. At the end comes a letter in which the Baal Shem Tov wrote in advance what would happen to the rabbi of Sde Lavan that winter.

## 9 — The Book of the Zohar, and the Sigh in Prayer

I heard from the great rabbi, the pious man of our community, Rabbi Gedaliah: once the Baal Shem Tov asked the rabbi of Kitov to lend him a book of the Zohar. On the way home his brother-in-law, Rabbi Gershon, met him and asked him: "What do you have under your coat?" The Baal Shem Tov did not want to tell him. Rabbi Gershon got down from the wagon, pulled the book of the Zohar out from under his coat, took it from him, and said in astonishment: "You need a book of the Zohar?!"

Afterward the Baal Shem Tov came to town to pray in the study house, and during the Shema he sighed a great sigh. After the prayer Rabbi Moshe, the head of the town's rabbinical court, asked him: "What was that great sigh I heard from you during prayer?" He coaxed him with words, and the Baal Shem Tov told him the truth, that this sigh had come from the heart, and said to him: "The mezuzah is unfit." They examined it and found it as he had said. Rabbi Moshe gave him the book of the Zohar, and blessed him that Rabbi Gershon would not harm him.

**Summary:** Rabbi Gershon took from the Baal Shem Tov a book of the Zohar he had borrowed from the rabbi of Kitov, and asked him in astonishment: "You need a book of the Zohar?" Afterward the rabbi of Kitov heard the Baal Shem Tov sigh in prayer, and the Baal Shem Tov told him that the mezuzah was unfit, and so it turned out. The rabbi gave him the book of the Zohar, and blessed him that Rabbi Gershon would not harm him.

## 10 — The Flour for Passover, and Elijah the Prophet

I heard from Rabbi Gedaliah: once the Baal Shem Tov had neither matzah nor meat for Passover. He harnessed his donkey to the wagon and went out to the villages. In the villages he slaughtered, and brought flour and meat for Passover. When he arrived, he said to his wife: "See that they unload the flour from the wagon." Rain fell, and the flour became chametz, and she told her husband. He checked his knife, went to the villages on the other side, and brought flour for the festival.

As he came near his home, there was a big hill there, and the horse could not pull the wagon. The Baal Shem Tov pulled the wagon himself to help the horse. Halfway up the hill the horse died, and the Baal Shem Tov had to pull the wagon by himself, for he was afraid to leave the flour unguarded. His strength gave out, and he stood by the wagon and wept a great weeping before God. As he wept, sleep fell on him and he fell asleep, and Elijah the Prophet came to him in a dream. This was the first time Elijah revealed himself to him. Elijah said to him: "Your tear has been accepted, and your prayer has been heard. I will send you a gentile, who will bring your wagon with the flour to your home." As soon as he woke, a gentile came with a wagon and horses, and said: "Israel, tie your wagon to mine, and I will bring you home."

When they reached his home, the gentile said: "What will you give me if I skin your horse?" And he gave him a coin. Afterward a gentile came and bought the hide for four zlotys, and the buyer said to him: "With this money, see that you clothe yourself and your wife for the next festival." In time caravans of merchants brought marten skins. His wife went to her husband and asked him whether to buy or not. (In the winter the Baal Shem Tov had a house of seclusion in the village, with a gentile from whom he rented a house there.) He said to her: "You may buy, for it is ownerless: robbers will kill them all." And she bought with this money, four zlotys. The Baal Shem Tov made himself a fur coat, and for his wife a dress for the festival, of *biliye*.

When the tollkeeper from whom the Baal Shem Tov leased the tavern saw that the Baal Shem Tov had clothed himself and his wife, he suspected that he was buying vodka somewhere else and not from him. The tollkeeper complained to Rabbi Gershon and said: "I respect you, but if he does not admit it and pay me for my loss, I will see to it that he is flogged at the fortress." Rabbi Gershon said to him: "I will reprove him." When the Baal Shem Tov came to town, Rabbi Gershon reproved him, and the Baal Shem Tov said to him: "Heaven forbid, such a thing never happened." Rabbi Gershon said: "Where do you have money from to clothe yourself and your wife?" He answered him: "God gave it to me." Rabbi Gershon said: "And why does God not give to me?" He answered him: "To you He does not give, and to me He gives." He said to him: "You will see, the tollkeeper will inform on you at the fortress, and you will be flogged." The Baal Shem Tov said: "I am not afraid of him." And in the end, when the tollkeeper wanted to inform on him, he died.

**Summary:** Before Passover the Baal Shem Tov was left without flour, because the flour he had brought became chametz in the rain, and on the way back with new flour his horse died halfway up the hill. He wept before God, and Elijah the Prophet revealed himself to him for the first time and sent him a gentile who brought him home. With the money for the horse's hide they bought furs and made clothes for the festival, and the tollkeeper suspected that the Baal Shem Tov was buying vodka from someone else and threatened to inform on him, but the tollkeeper died.

## 11 — The Horse That Came Back

I heard from my father-in-law: the Baal Shem Tov had a horse, and it was stolen from him. Rabbi Gershon reproved him, and in the middle of it a sentence suddenly slipped out of his mouth: "They will bring the horse back to him." Rabbi Gershon laughed at him, and every time he came to town he would tease him and say to him: "Israel, have they brought your horse back?" The horse was missing for about a year. In the end a gentile came riding on that very horse, and knocked at the window so that they would bring him a light for his pipe, and the Baal Shem Tov recognized his horse and said: "But that is my horse!" At once the gentile got down from the horse and gave it back to him.

**Summary:** The Baal Shem Tov's horse was stolen, and it slipped out of his mouth that it would be brought back to him, and Rabbi Gershon mocked him about it for a year. In the end a gentile riding the horse knocked at the window and asked for a light for his pipe. The Baal Shem Tov recognized the horse, and the gentile got down at once and gave it back to him.

## 12 — The Light Under the Stove

I heard from Rabbi Gedaliah, and also from my father-in-law, something that happened. Rabbi David, the preacher of Kolomea, was traveling to collect "Chanukah money," lost his way, and came to the Baal Shem Tov's house while the Baal Shem Tov was sitting in his house of seclusion. Rabbi David asked his wife where her husband was, and she answered that he had to go to the tollkeeper to help him water the animals. The preacher said: "What can I eat here?" She answered him: "My husband slaughtered a fowl. Check the knife, and if it satisfies you, I will cook the hens." He checked the knife, and it satisfied him.

The righteous woman went and told her husband. The Baal Shem Tov came home, and pretended that he too was coming from the tollkeeper. He served the preacher as a lesser man serves a greater one: he made up the bed for him himself, and set out water for him and a basin to pour the water into. And he and his wife lay down to sleep in one bed, as is the custom of village people.

At midnight the Baal Shem Tov got up (I heard that he slept only two hours), and sat quietly under the stove, and quietly occupied himself with what he occupied himself with. Rabbi David the preacher woke up, and saw a great light under the stove. He thought the kindling splinters on the stove had caught fire and were burning, and he began to wake the Baal Shem Tov's wife: "Chana, Chana, a fire is burning on the stove!" She answered him: "I am a woman. By the time I get dressed and cover my head with a kerchief, the fire will burn more and grow stronger. Let the rabbi forgive me, he has water by him, and he can put it out himself." He took the basin with the water that had been poured out, and went to put out the fire. When he reached the stove, he saw the Baal Shem Tov sitting, with light shining on him. (And the rabbi told in confidence that the light stood above him like a rainbow.) When the preacher saw this, he fainted, and Chana had to wake the Baal Shem Tov and disturb him, so that he would wake the preacher from his faint.

In the morning Rabbi David the preacher said: "This and this is what I saw. Tell me, what is it?" The Baal Shem Tov answered him: "I do not know. I was saying Psalms; perhaps I cleaved to God, and that is what happened." Rabbi David decreed that he reveal it to him, and the Baal Shem Tov revealed himself to him. From that day on Rabbi David the preacher traveled to him every time to hear words of Torah from him. When his colleagues heard this Torah from him, they asked: "Where do you have this from?" And he answered: "From a certain poor and needy man." And from then on, when Rabbi David heard that Rabbi Gershon was reproving the Baal Shem Tov and insulting him, he said to him: "Leave him alone, for he is wiser than you." (For the Baal Shem Tov had decreed that Rabbi David must not reveal it.)

**Summary:** The preacher Rabbi David of Kolomea lost his way and spent the night in the house of the Baal Shem Tov, who pretended to be a simple villager and served him. At midnight he saw a great light under the stove, thought a fire had broken out, found the Baal Shem Tov sitting with light shining on him, and fainted. The Baal Shem Tov had to reveal himself to him, and from then on Rabbi David traveled to him to hear Torah, and when Rabbi Gershon insulted the Baal Shem Tov, Rabbi David told him that he was wiser than he was.

## 13 — The Dream of the Palace

In Mezhibuzh there was a rich man who did not like the Baal Shem Tov. In his house there was a teacher, a great scholar and wise in all his ways, and he too kept away from the Baal Shem Tov. The Baal Shem Tov very much wanted to bring him close to the service of God, for he was ready for every good thing, but he wondered how this could come about, since the teacher kept very far away. All the more so because the atmosphere of the house he lived in also served him as an excellent guard.

Once, on a Friday night, the teacher dreamed that he was walking all through the town of Mezhibuzh, and he saw a beautiful, splendid palace, as splendid as could possibly be. He was greatly amazed, and could not have his fill of looking, for the more he looked, the more wonders he saw. Afterward he thought: "All this splendor is only from the building materials themselves. And what will it be when I look at the beauty of the workmanship and the paintings?" And when he looked deeply at the beauty of the workmanship, he was greatly amazed, for in literally every small space there was wisdom and craft whose like had never been seen in the whole world. His heart saw much wisdom, and was drawn after it with strong longing. And again he said in his heart: "If the outside of the palace is so splendid, the inside must surely be beyond measure." He went up to a window to look inside, and there were the Baal Shem Tov and all his holy circle sitting around the table, and the Baal Shem Tov was saying words of Torah. His heart burned to go inside, and he ran to the door. When he wanted to go in, the servant pushed him away and did not let him in, and he was very sorry. Even so, out of his great eagerness and desire to hear the words of the living God, he stood by the window and heard all his holy words.

He woke up, and it was a dream. He began to go over the words of Torah he had heard, and they were sweeter than honey. He went over them two and three times. But since it was still about midnight, he had to go back to sleep. In the morning, when he got out of bed, he remembered the dream with perfect clarity, but the words of Torah he had forgotten completely. He was so sorry about it that he was truly confused from all his sorrow, and did not know how he had prayed. At the morning meal he sat stunned. The master of the house said to him: "What has happened today? If you dreamed a bad dream, we will have the dream turned to good for you before three friends." And the teacher did not answer him. Perhaps he would have gone to the Baal Shem Tov himself, but he remembered how the servant had pushed him away in the dream, and he was very afraid that the same would happen in reality, and that shame was enough for him. So he was in great sorrow all day.

At the third Sabbath meal the Baal Shem Tov commanded: "Go to the house of the rich man you know of, and call the teacher to come here to me." And everyone was greatly surprised. But as soon as the messenger opened the door and said to the teacher: "The Baal Shem Tov is calling you to come to him," the teacher jumped up from the table and ran like a madman, without a coat. And there he heard all the words of Torah he had heard in the night, and at once he fainted. When his spirit came back to him, the Baal Shem Tov said to him with a smile: "If you had heard something new, I would understand your being so moved. But it is nothing new, for you already heard all this in the night." And the teacher understood that this was the word of God, and from then on he drew close to him with all his heart, and became a complete tzaddik.

**Summary:** A learned teacher who lived in the house of a rich man, one of the Baal Shem Tov's opponents, kept away from him. On a Friday night he dreamed that he was standing by the window of a wondrous palace and hearing the Baal Shem Tov say Torah, but in the morning he had forgotten it all and was very sorry. At the third Sabbath meal the Baal Shem Tov called for him, and the teacher ran to him and heard exactly the same Torah again, and fainted. From then on he drew close to the Baal Shem Tov with all his heart, and became a complete tzaddik.

## 14 — The Son Who Traveled to Breslau

In that town there was a great merchant, one of those who were still against the Baal Shem Tov. The merchant had an only son, wise and great in learning and in other virtues. Once the son traveled to Breslau to buy goods. Usually such a journey took four weeks, but this time more than ten weeks passed, and no news came from him, and the family was very worried. His mother, the merchant's wife, said: "Why do you harden your heart against the Baal Shem Tov? The whole world travels to him, literally from the four corners of the earth, and he does things for them, and you do not want to go to his house to ask him about our son? It is not an empty thing." He paid no attention to her words. But in time she pressed him hard, and he had to do as she wished.

On the Sabbath eve he went to the Baal Shem Tov and said: "Rebbe, it is already more than ten weeks since my dear son traveled to Breslau, and I have no news of him, and we do not know what has happened to him." At once the Baal Shem Tov ordered that they bring him a book of the Zohar, opened it, looked into it, and answered him: "Your son, thank God, is alive and well. And this Sabbath he will spend in a village near our town, a parsah away." The merchant did not believe his words: "How can that be? My son knows how much we long for him, and surely, just as we long for him, so he longs for us. Surely money does not matter to him, and he will pay any price for a wagon to get home for the Sabbath." Even so, to be safe, he sent a gentile to that village, and ordered him to wait there until nightfall; perhaps the son would arrive before nightfall. At night the gentile came back and said: "Not a sound, not an answer. They have heard nothing and seen nothing of your son." The merchant said to his wife: "This is exactly what I told you before: all his words are empty enticements with nothing to them."

But in truth, this is what happened to the son: his wagon broke down several times on the way, and he reached that village at midnight. And right at the end of the Sabbath the son came home. Then the merchant saw that all the Baal Shem Tov's words had been true and right. He went to the Baal Shem Tov, asked his forgiveness for having spoken evil of him, and told him the whole story. The Baal Shem Tov said to him: "Fool! The light that God created in the six days of creation, by it a person could look from one end of the world to the other, and God hid it away for the righteous in the time to come. And where did He hide it? In the Torah. And so when I open the book of the Zohar, I see the whole world, and with God's help I will not be mistaken in what I see."

**Summary:** A merchant, one of the Baal Shem Tov's opponents, had heard nothing for ten weeks from his son who had traveled to Breslau, and under pressure from his wife he asked the Baal Shem Tov. The Baal Shem Tov looked into the book of the Zohar and said that the son was alive and would spend the Sabbath in a nearby village, but the gentile sent to the village did not find him, and the merchant said the Baal Shem Tov spoke empty words. It turned out that the son's wagon had broken down and he had reached the village at midnight, and the merchant asked forgiveness. The Baal Shem Tov explained to him that the hidden light, by which one sees from one end of the world to the other, is in the Torah, and so in the Zohar he sees the whole world.

## 15 — The Trembling in Prayer

I heard from the famous pious Rabbi Falk of Tetchelnik, who heard it from Rabbi Avraham, the head of the rabbinical court of Dubossar, who had earlier been the cantor of the study house in Mezhibuzh: once they had to say Hallel (it was Rosh Chodesh, or the intermediate days of Passover). Rabbi Avraham led the morning prayer before the ark, and the Baal Shem Tov prayed in his place. The Baal Shem Tov's custom was to lead the prayers before the ark himself from Hallel onward. During the cantor's repetition the Baal Shem Tov began to tremble greatly, and went on trembling, as was his custom always to tremble in prayer (whoever saw him at prayer saw his trembling). When Rabbi Avraham finished the cantor's repetition, the Baal Shem Tov stood in his place and trembled, and did not go to the ark. The pious Rabbi Zev Kutzes came and looked into his face: his face was burning like torches, and his eyes were bulging and open, and stood without moving, like those of a dying man. Rabbi Zev signaled to Rabbi Avraham, and the two of them took the Baal Shem Tov, one by one hand and the other by the other hand, and led him to the ark. He went with them, stood before the ark and trembled for a while, and then began and said Hallel, all of it trembling. Afterward, when he had finished the Kaddish, he stood and trembled for a long time, and they had to wait with the Torah reading until his trembling calmed down.

And not only that. I heard from the Rabbi of Polonne, the author of the book *Toldot Yaakov Yosef*, that once a large vessel of water stood there while the Baal Shem Tov was praying, and they saw that the water was moving and shaking. The Shechinah truly rested on him, and from this the earth trembled, as it is written: "Because the Lord descended upon it in fire, and the whole mountain trembled greatly." In the ground it could not be seen, but in the water it could be seen very clearly.

I also heard from the preacher Rabbi Eliyahu: when he came for the first time to the great Maggid of Mezritch, the Maggid told him this story: "Once, on a festival (I do not know whether it was the first day of Passover or Shemini Atzeret, when one must mention rain or dew), the Baal Shem Tov led the prayers before the ark with great fervor." (So I heard many times from his choice disciples: he was taller than all of them, and prayed with a great cry.) "The great Maggid could not bear it, for he was very ill. He left the study house, went into a small room in the study house, and prayed there alone. Before Musaf the Baal Shem Tov came into the small room to put on his kittel." (The Maggid said that he recognized that the Shechinah was resting on him, and when he saw him, he recognized that he was not in this world.) "When he put on the kittel, the kittel was creased on his shoulders, and the great Maggid took hold of the kittel to straighten it. When he touched it, he began to tremble, and he held on to the table that stood there, and the table too began to tremble together with him. And the Baal Shem Tov went out. And in the end the Maggid had to pray and ask God to take this thing away from him, for he did not have the strength to bear it."

I also heard from the pious Rabbi David Purkes of Mezhibuzh that once the Baal Shem Tov prayed while traveling, in a house, by the eastern wall. By the western wall stood barrels full of grain, and they saw that the grain was trembling.

I also heard from him, though not directly from his mouth: when I was a shochet in Tulchin, Rabbi David of the village of Chladovka held an engagement, and sent wagons to bring all the invited guests. Rabbi David the preacher also traveled to the engagement, and on the way he told a story. And when the people of the community came, they told it to me:

Once, on Yom Kippur, the Baal Shem Tov did not come to the study house early, as people do on Yom Kippur, praying early. He was very, very late, and they waited for him and did not begin to pray. Afterward he came, and sat in his place, with his head on the lectern: he raised his head and laid it down, raised it and laid it down, several times. Afterward he signaled that they should go and lead the prayers before the ark. Rabbi David came to lead the prayers, for he was the cantor on the Days of Awe. When he came near the ark, the Baal Shem Tov began to shame him before the whole congregation, and said this: "Old sinner, where are you going?" And he shamed and humiliated him for about half an hour. Rabbi David wanted to turn back and leave the prayer stand, for he was very hurt, and he thought: "Perhaps he saw something shameful in me, Heaven forbid." But the Baal Shem Tov rebuked him and said: "Stand!" And he began to pray, and wept, and did not know what he was saying; he only cried out and wept, for his heart was very broken.

At the end of Yom Kippur Rabbi David the preacher went to the Baal Shem Tov and said to him: "Our master, what shameful thing did you see in me, Heaven forbid?" He answered him: "Heaven forbid, I saw nothing in you. But before dawn I saw the Satan standing on the way, in the place where the prayers go up, to take all the prayers, Heaven forbid. I said in my heart: why should I go and pray and hand the prayers over to him? And so I was late to pray. And thank God, I cleared one way, on which the prayers would go and he would not be able to catch them. And so I was afraid that the Satan might enter your heart and spoil the matter, and I broke your heart, so that there would be no stray thought in your heart, and only then did I command you to pray."

**Summary:** The Baal Shem Tov trembled in his prayer so much that once, after the cantor's repetition, he stood without moving, his face burning, and two men led him to the ark to say Hallel. When he prayed, even the water in a vessel and the grain in the barrels trembled, and the Maggid of Mezritch, who touched his kittel, trembled until he asked God to make it stop. One Yom Kippur the Baal Shem Tov insulted the cantor Rabbi David before everyone, and afterward explained that he had seen the Satan lying in wait for the prayers, and had broken Rabbi David's heart so that he would have no stray thought.

## 16 — The Barrels That Danced, and the Dream of the Altar

I also heard from the pious rabbi Rabbi Gedaliah: once the Baal Shem Tov prayed the afternoon prayer in a village, in a room called a granary. There were many barrels full of grain there, and during the prayer there was a great dancing among the barrels, because of the trembling, as it is written: "Because the Lord descended upon it in fire, and the whole mountain trembled greatly." And thank God, I saw in this an aspect of the soul of Moses our teacher.

I also heard from Rabbi Falk. He went into exile to the Crimean peninsula with Rabbi Yosef Ashkenazi, the Baal Shem Tov's cantor, at the time of the flight, and heard this story from him, and I heard it from Rabbi Falk. Once Rabbi Yosef dreamed that he saw a certain Hasid who had passed away. The Hasid had left a small son, a clever child, and a match had been proposed for the son with a simple, lowly family. The Hasid came to Rabbi Yosef in the dream, and asked him to go to his wife and tell her in his name not to make this match, for he did not want it: "Only with the man who comes to her house first today, with him she should make a match." Afterward he saw in the dream something like an altar, and the departed man went up on the altar, put his head between his knees, and began to cry out the penitential prayer "Answer us, Lord, answer us; answer us, our Father, answer us," etc., through the whole alphabet to the end. Afterward he said: "Answer us, God of our fathers, answer us; answer us, God of Abraham, answer us; answer us, Fear of Isaac, answer us; answer us, Mighty One of Jacob, answer us; answer us, You who understand tears, answer us; answer us, God of the Chariot, answer us," and flew up to the heavens.

In the morning Rabbi Yosef went to the Baal Shem Tov and told him the dream. The Baal Shem Tov said to him: "You think you dreamed a dream? No! You saw a true vision. I saw it too, and it truly happened before my eyes. And the altar is the altar on which the angel Michael offers the souls before God." Rabbi Yosef asked him: "Why did he say these 'Answer us' prayers?" He answered him: "Because an angel and souls cannot rise from world to world except through the 'Answer us' prayers." He asked him: "And why did he say them in this version?" He answered him: "Because above, that is the version." And the Baal Shem Tov sent for the dead man's wife, and told her the words about the match.

**Summary:** When the Baal Shem Tov prayed the afternoon prayer in a granary, the barrels danced from the trembling. His cantor, Rabbi Yosef Ashkenazi, dreamed that a departed Hasid asked him to warn his wife not to make a match for their son with a simple family, and afterward he saw the dead man go up on an altar, say "Answer us," and fly up to heaven. The Baal Shem Tov told him that it was not a dream but a true vision that he had seen too, explained what the altar was and why he said "Answer us," and sent to tell the widow about the match.

## 17 — The Locked Gate on Yom Kippur

I also heard from him (from Rabbi Falk): once, on the eve of Yom Kippur, the Baal Shem Tov saw a great accusation against the Jewish people, that the Oral Torah would be taken from them. He was very sad the whole eve of Yom Kippur. Toward evening, when all the people of the town came to receive his blessing, he blessed one or two, and said: "I cannot, from too much sorrow," and did not bless them. He went to the synagogue and spoke harsh words of reproof before them. He fell upon the holy ark, cried out and said: "Oh, they want to take the Torah out of our hands! How will we be able to survive among the nations even half a day?" He was very angry with the rabbis, and said that it was because of them, for they invent falsehoods out of their own hearts, with false premises. And he said that all the sages of the Mishnah and the Talmud stood in judgment. Afterward he went to the study house and spoke more words of reproof, and they prayed Kol Nidrei.

After Kol Nidrei he said that the accusation was growing stronger. So he hurried all the cantors along, so that he could stand up for the Ne'ilah prayer while it was still broad day, for he himself always led the Ne'ilah prayer. Before Ne'ilah he began to speak words of reproof, and wept, and laid his head back on the ark, and groaned and cried out. Afterward he began to pray the Amidah silently, and afterward the repetition aloud. And his custom on the Days of Awe was not to look in the prayer book: Rabbi Yakil of Mezhibuzh would say the liturgical poems before him from the prayer book, and the Baal Shem Tov would repeat after him. When they came to the words "Open the gate for us" or "Open the gates of heaven," Rabbi Yakil said them once and again, and heard that the Baal Shem Tov was not repeating after him, and fell silent. The Baal Shem Tov began to make terrible movements: he bent backward until his head came close to his knees. The congregation was afraid he would fall to the ground, and wanted to catch him and hold him up, but they were afraid. They told Rabbi Zev Kutzes, and he came, looked into his face, and signaled that they should not touch him. His eyes were bulging, and his voice made sounds like a slaughtered ox. So it went for about two hours. Suddenly he came to himself, stood up straight, prayed with great speed and finished the prayer.

At the end of Yom Kippur everyone came to greet him, as was always the custom, and asked him what had happened with the accusation. He told them: "In the Ne'ilah prayer I was able to pray, and I went from world to world without any hindrance, through the whole silent Amidah. And in the repetition aloud I went on as well, until I came to a palace, and I had only one more gate to go through in order to come before God. In that palace I found prayers of fifty years that had not been able to rise. And now, because we prayed with intention on this Yom Kippur, all the prayers rose, and every prayer shone like the light of dawn. I said to these prayers: 'Why did you not rise before?' And they said: 'So we were commanded, to wait for your honor to lead us.' I said to them: 'Come with me.' And the gate was open." (And to the people of his town he said that the gate was as big as the whole world.)

"When we began to go with the prayers, an angel came, shut the door, and hung a lock on the gate." (And he told them that the lock was as big as the whole of Mezhibuzh.) "I began to turn the lock to open it, and I could not. I ran to my master, the one known from the book *Toldot Yaakov Yosef*, and pleaded with him. I said: 'The Jewish people are in such great trouble, and now they will not let me in! At another time I would not push myself to go in.' My master said: 'I will go with you, and if it is possible to open it for you, I will open it.' He came and turned the lock, and he too could not open it. Then he said: 'What can I do for you?' I began to complain bitterly before my master: 'How could you leave me in such a time of trouble?' He answered me: 'I do not know what to do for you. But you and I will go to the palace of the Messiah; perhaps salvation will come from there.' I went with a great tumult to the palace of the Messiah. When our righteous Messiah saw me from afar, he said to me: 'Do not cry out.' And he gave me two letters. I came to the gate, and thank God, I opened the lock, and opened the gate, and led in all the prayers. And from the great joy that all the prayers had risen, the mouth of the accuser was stopped, and I did not need to plead, and the decree was canceled. Only a trace of the decree remained."

And of that trace I remember that there was a great uproar in the world, for many people sent their books away to the land of Wallachia. This accusation came from the sect of Shabbetai Tzvi, may his name be blotted out, and a bishop of the city of Kamenets burned two volumes of the Gemara. The rabbi of our community said that the bishop took a Talmud by force from the tollkeeper of his village, paid him for it, put it on the fire, and because of our many sins it was all burned. And thank God, as soon as the bishop stood before the fire, he was struck, and they carried him to the city of Kamenets, and they did not bring him there alive: that wicked man died on the way. Afterward there was a debate before the bishop of Lvov, but the fear of the Torah fell on him, and he did not condemn it. And the people of that wicked sect all converted, for they had been put to great shame and disgrace: one sidelock and half the beard were shaved off them, so that people would recognize that they were neither Jews nor gentiles. And out of great shame they converted.

But once the pious Rabbi Menachem Nachum of Chernobyl was in our town, and told this story before the congregation. I arrived in the middle of the story, and heard from him a small difference, and also something new: that the Messiah said to the Baal Shem Tov: "I do not know whether you will open it. But if you open it, there will surely be salvation for the Jewish people." Rabbi Menachem Nachum also said that this gate was the gate of the "Palace of the Bird's Nest," which no one has ever entered except the Messiah, as it is written in the holy Zohar. And he also said that the Baal Shem Tov heard the voice of God saying to him: "What shall I do with you, that I must do your will?"

And from the words of the rabbi of our community it seems that the Baal Shem Tov was still in sorrow, and was angry with the guests who had left his house. Afterward he sent a messenger, and the messenger heard that his wife reproved him for being angry with the guests, and he leaned on the table and said: "I am ready to accept reproof." And afterward he sent for the guests, and in the morning he stood up to pray with the congregation.

And about those who converted, I heard from the rabbi of our community that the Baal Shem Tov said: "The Shechinah wails and says: as long as a limb is attached to the body, there is hope that it will be healed. But when the limb is cut off, it can never be repaired. For each one of the Jewish people is a limb of the Shechinah."

**Summary:** On the eve of Yom Kippur the Baal Shem Tov saw a great accusation, that the Oral Torah would be taken from the Jewish people, and he prayed all day with weeping and terrible movements. In the Ne'ilah prayer he rose from world to world with prayers of fifty years, and found a locked gate that even his master could not open, until the Messiah gave him two letters, and with them he opened the gate and the decree was canceled. Only a trace of the decree remained: because of the sect of Shabbetai Tzvi a bishop in Kamenets burned volumes of the Talmud, and the bishop died on the way, and the people of the sect converted. The Baal Shem Tov said that the Shechinah wails over every Jew who is cut off from her, for every Jew is a limb of the Shechinah.

## 18 — The Town of Balta

I heard from Rabbi Shmeril, the preacher of the town of Verkhivka, father-in-law of the pious Rabbi Falk the Kohen: once the Baal Shem Tov spent the Sabbath in the town of Balta, and said that this town would one day be destroyed, and afterward it would be rebuilt and become "a mother city in Israel" (a great Jewish city), and would stand until the redeemer comes, speedily in our days. The rabbi of our community explained how the Baal Shem Tov came to say this: in Balta there were great fairs, and most of the merchants there were gentiles: Turks, Tatars and people of other nations. At the third Sabbath meal, while the Baal Shem Tov was saying Torah, they raced past in wagons and disturbed him. He grew angry and said: "What is with you, Balta? Trouble will yet spring up for you!"

**Summary:** When the Baal Shem Tov spent the Sabbath in the town of Balta, gentile merchants raced past in wagons while he was saying Torah at the third Sabbath meal and disturbed him. He grew angry and said that trouble would yet come upon the town, and that it would be destroyed and afterward rebuilt and become a great Jewish city until the coming of the redeemer.

## 19 — The Destruction of Balta

I heard from Rabbi Shmeril, one year before the flight, that when there was a war between the Turks and Greece, the town of Balta was destroyed, and all the area around it too, and also the town of Tetchelnik and all the villages around it. In Balta nothing was left but one small house opposite the courtyard.

**Summary:** Rabbi Shmeril told that in the war between the Turks and Greece the town of Balta and all the area around it were destroyed, and only one small house was left in it, as the Baal Shem Tov had said.

## 20 — The Souls That Came During the Afternoon Prayer

I heard from Rabbi Falk: when Rabbi Gershon of Kitov traveled from the Land of Israel to the lands abroad to arrange matches for his sons, he said on the way: "Thank God I have crossed the sea. I will travel to my brother-in-law, the Baal Shem Tov." He came to him on the Sabbath eve. The Baal Shem Tov stood up to pray the afternoon prayer, and drew out his prayer until the stars came out. Rabbi Gershon prayed too, from the prayer book of the Arizal, and afterward read the weekly portion "twice in the Hebrew and once in the Aramaic translation," and afterward asked that they bring him pillows, and lay down to rest.

On Friday night, during the meal, Rabbi Gershon asked his brother-in-law: "Why did you draw out your prayer so long? I too prayed with the mystical intentions, and read the portion twice in the Hebrew and once in the translation, and I had to lie down and rest, and you stand and tremble and make your movements." (He wanted to draw things out of him.) The Baal Shem Tov laughed at him, and did not answer him at all. Rabbi Gershon insisted and asked again. Then the Baal Shem Tov answered him: "When I reach the words 'who revives the dead' (Rabbi Falk was not sure whether it was 'You revive the dead' or 'who revives the dead with mercy'), I concentrate on mystical intentions of unification. And then souls of the dead come, thousands and tens of thousands, and I have to speak with each and every one: why was it pushed away from its place? I make a *tikkun* for it, and pray for it, and raise it up, the more important first. And they are so many that if I wanted to raise them all, I would have to stand in the Amidah for three years. But when I hear the heavenly proclamation announcing 'Holy from holy,' and no more souls can be raised, I step back and finish the Amidah."

Rabbi Gershon said to him jokingly: "Why do they not come to me?" He answered him: "Stay with me another Sabbath, and I will give you the mystical intentions in writing on paper, and they will come to you too." And so he did. On the next Sabbath eve, when the Baal Shem Tov finished the Kaddish before the prayer, Rabbi Gershon too stood up to pray. The Baal Shem Tov did not yet begin to pray, for he knew that Rabbi Gershon would not withstand it and would be frightened. Meanwhile he adjusted his watch and took snuff, until Rabbi Gershon would pass the place of the intention. When Rabbi Gershon concentrated on this intention, he saw the dead coming to him like a great flock of sheep, and he fainted. Then the Baal Shem Tov woke him, and ordered him to go home. At night, during the meal, the Baal Shem Tov asked him: "Why did you faint?" He said to him: "When I concentrated on the intention, the dead came to me like flocks." Then the Baal Shem Tov said to his people jokingly: "Give him a beating, so that he will not laugh at the Baal Shem Tov anymore!"

**Summary:** Rabbi Gershon asked the Baal Shem Tov why he drew out the afternoon prayer of the Sabbath eve so long, and the Baal Shem Tov revealed to him that during the prayer thousands of souls of the dead come to him, and he repairs them and raises them up until the Sabbath comes in. Rabbi Gershon asked jokingly why they did not come to him, and the Baal Shem Tov gave him the mystical intentions in writing. When Rabbi Gershon concentrated on them, the dead came to him like a flock of sheep, and he fainted.

## 21 — The Rabbi of Shargorod and the Mokhiach

I heard from the pious, wise and famous Rabbi of Polonne, who was head of the rabbinical court in Shargorod. He heard that the Baal Shem Tov had come to the town of Mohilev, and said in his heart: "I will go too," for he was not yet a Hasid. He traveled and reached him on the Sabbath eve, before the morning prayer. He saw that the Baal Shem Tov was smoking a pipe, and this seemed strange to him. "And afterward, during the prayer, I wept a great weeping, such as I had never wept in all my days, and I understood that this weeping was not my own. Afterward the Baal Shem Tov traveled to the Land of Israel, and I remained stunned until he came back. Then I began to travel to him, and I stayed with him for a while, and the Baal Shem Tov said that I needed to be raised up. I stayed about five weeks, and I asked: 'When will your honor raise me up?'" And the rest I heard from the rabbi of our community, Rabbi Gedaliah.

Rabbi Gedaliah told that he heard from that rabbi: the Mokhiach was the first of the Baal Shem Tov's messengers. He came to him in Shargorod for the Sabbath, and asked to stay with him, and the rabbi did not turn him away empty-handed. The Mokhiach asked him to make a minyan for him in his house to pray, and the rabbi gave him permission, but he himself went to the synagogue. On the Sabbath, when the Mokhiach went to preach, he asked the rabbi to come to the sermon. And in the sermon he "clothed" the rabbi from the soles of his feet to his head: everything he said fit the rabbi exactly. The rabbi said in his heart: "This can only be a prophet, for how does he know my thoughts?" And he clung to him. At once he invited the people of the community to the third Sabbath meal, and they were surprised, for they knew that he did not think much of Hasidim.

Afterward, as the rabbi grew stronger in his Hasidic way, the people of the town began to quarrel with him, and became his great enemies. The dispute grew stronger and stronger, until they drove him out of the town on the Sabbath eve, and he had to spend the Sabbath in a village. The Baal Shem Tov was traveling, not far from there, and when he saw what had happened, he said to the people close to him: "Let us go to that village, for I know that the rabbi is in great sorrow, and let us spend the Sabbath there with the rabbi of Shargorod, to ease his sorrow." And so they did. The Mokhiach was there with them too. On the Sabbath, after the Musaf prayer, the Mokhiach saw that the rabbi was in sorrow, and said to him: "Do not be sad. I heard a proclamation in Heaven that announced: So-and-so, your enemy, will be killed; and so-and-so, your enemy, will die on the road; and the whole town will be burned with fire." When the Baal Shem Tov heard his words, he shouted at the Mokhiach and said: "Fool! You too hear proclamations?" And the Mokhiach was silent.

The rabbi traveled to the town of Rashkov, and they took him as rabbi there. There he repented a great repentance. He was rich, he had a fortune of sixty thousand, and he gave away all his money: he returned all the fines he had received, and from any business that had even a trace of something forbidden in it, he returned the money. He proclaimed under a ban that anyone from whom the rabbi had taken money without permission should come and take his money back, until the rabbi was left poor.

**Summary:** The rabbi of Shargorod came to the Baal Shem Tov even before he was a Hasid, and wept in prayer a weeping he felt was not his own. The Mokhiach, a messenger of the Baal Shem Tov, preached a sermon in the town that described the rabbi's thoughts exactly, and the rabbi drew close to Hasidism, and the people of the town quarreled with him and drove him out on the Sabbath eve. The Baal Shem Tov came to spend the Sabbath with him in the village, and rebuked the Mokhiach for revealing aloud a proclamation he had heard in Heaven about the punishment of the enemies and the burning of the town. Afterward the rabbi became rabbi of Rashkov, returned any money that was in doubt, and was left poor.

## 22 — The Tallit Given as a Pledge, and the Fasts

I heard from Rabbi Yoel, the preacher of Nemirov, whose burial place is in Jerusalem: once he traveled with the rabbi of Rashkov to Mezhibuzh, and they came to the town of Bar. They prayed there with a minyan at the inn, and at the end of the prayer the rabbi disappeared from among them. They went to look for him, and found him coming out of a house. They asked the owner of the house, who was a goldsmith: "What was the rabbi doing in your house?" And he did not want to reveal it, because the rabbi had decreed that he must not reveal it. The important men who were there with the rabbi were angry with him, and decreed that the goldsmith tell them the truth. The goldsmith said: "I see revenge in it. When the rabbi lived in Rashkov, he took a fine of two zlotys from me. And now he gave me his Sabbath tallit (as a pledge), until he comes back from his journey and redeems it." At once the men decreed upon him: "Forgive him the two zlotys at once, and give him back the tallit!" The goldsmith gave back the tallit, but the rabbi did not want to accept it, until the goldsmith shook his hand on it that he forgave him with a whole heart. Only then did he accept the tallit.

And about the rabbi's fasts: he fasted according to the order of repentance in the book *HaKanah*, in this way: he fasted every day, and once a month he fasted from Sabbath to Sabbath. No one in his household knew about the fasts, except one girl, the daughter of the rabbi's sister. He arranged with her that she, and no one else, would bring him food, and also clear the food away, so that the household would not know. So he fasted for five years. In the sixth year he once fasted from Sabbath to Sabbath, and the Baal Shem Tov, who was traveling, heard a proclamation: "Hurry, run to him, for today his mind will be lost!" And the Baal Shem Tov ran to him with great speed, until a horse worth ten zlotys died on him on the way. He said to the rabbi: "Because of you a horse of ten zlotys died on me on the way, and it will be an atonement in your place." And at once the rabbi ate, and from then on he listened to him.

I have in my hands a letter that the Baal Shem Tov sent to the rabbi. On the back of the letter was written: "From holy Mezhibuzh to great Nemirov, to the Kohen, the head of the rabbinical court, the wondrous rabbi, Rabbi Yosef HaKohen." And these are the words of the letter:

"To my beloved friend, the great rabbi, famous for his piety, the perfect sage, who is bound to my heart, Rabbi Yosef HaKohen. I have received your letter, and I saw in its two top lines that your honor writes as if he must fast. And my insides trembled at the words. And I add, by the decree of the angels and together with the Holy One, blessed be He, and His Shechinah, that he must not put himself in such danger. For this is a deed of melancholy and sadness, and the Shechinah does not rest on a person out of sadness, but out of the joy of a mitzvah, as your honor knows from the things I have taught several times, and let these words be on his heart. And as for the thoughts that bring him to this, I advise you, and God will be with you, mighty man of valor: every single morning, during your study, cleave to the letters, in complete attachment to the service of the Creator, blessed be He and blessed be His name. And then the judgments will be sweetened at their root, and the judgments will be lightened from upon you. And do not hide yourself from your own flesh, Heaven forbid, by fasting more than is required and needed. And if you listen to my voice, God will be with you. And with this I will be brief, and say peace from me, who seeks your welfare, Israel Baal Shem Tov."

**Summary:** In the town of Bar the disciples discovered that the rabbi of Rashkov had secretly given his Sabbath tallit as a pledge to a goldsmith, in order to repay him a fine he had once taken from him, and the rabbi took the tallit back only after the goldsmith forgave him with a whole heart. The rabbi fasted in secret for years, until the Baal Shem Tov heard a proclamation in Heaven that the fasts would harm his mind, and ran to him so fast that his horse died on the way. The Baal Shem Tov wrote him a letter saying that he must not fast out of sadness, for the Shechinah rests only out of joy, and that he should cleave to the letters of the Torah in his study.

## 23 — The Sabbath in Shargorod

(The letter continues:) "And much peace to his only son, the wondrous rabbi, my friend, Rabbi Shimshon, and through him to his son, my friend Rabbi Hirtz, and to all of them, together and each one."

I heard from the rabbi of our community, Rabbi Gedaliah: several times the rabbi wanted to travel to the Holy Land, and the Baal Shem Tov told him not to go. He said to him: "Let this be a sign for you: every time you feel a wish to travel to the Holy Land, know for certain that there are judgments on the town, and the Satan is troubling you so that you will not pray for the town. So when you feel a wish to travel to the Holy Land, pray for the town."

Once the rabbi traveled to the Baal Shem Tov for the Sabbath, and had to pass through the town of Shargorod. He was very angry with the town, and did not want to stop in it. But since he had to give fodder to the horses, he had to stop. He did not get down from the wagon, and said: "The moment the horses have eaten, we will leave at once." When they went into the house, a very heavy rain began to fall, so that they could not travel. The rain did not stop, until the rabbi had to stay the night there against his will. In the morning too the rain did not stop, but grew stronger and stronger, until Thursday. When he saw that the rains were not stopping, he said: "Master of the world, perhaps You want me to spend the Sabbath here, and the rains will stop." And so it was: the rains stopped at once. And he prayed for the town the whole Sabbath.

The Baal Shem Tov saw that the rabbi was spending the Sabbath in Shargorod, and he knew that he was very angry with the town. He laughed, and said to the people close to him: "Look, the rabbi of Rashkov is spending the Sabbath in Shargorod," and laughed some more. After the Sabbath, when the rabbi came to the Baal Shem Tov, the Baal Shem Tov greeted him and laughed. The rabbi said to the Baal Shem Tov: "Rebbe, why are you laughing?" He said to him: "Because you were in Shargorod the whole Sabbath." The rabbi answered: "That is right," and nestled up to him like an only son, and told him that he had had to spend the Sabbath there because of the rains. Then the Baal Shem Tov answered him: "Do you think the Mokhiach lied, Heaven forbid? He truly heard the proclamation. And why was I angry with him? Because he let the words out of his mouth. Had he not let the words of the proclamation out of his mouth, I would have canceled the decree on the town completely that Sabbath. And now all I achieved is that they were given relief: the town will not burn all at once, but now this street and now that street, until the whole town has burned."

And so it was: the town burned several times. And those who had been sentenced to be killed were killed in the town of Uman at the time of the flight. And those who had been sentenced to die of plague died in the plague that came two years after the flight. And they had a long reprieve, about twenty years or more, for I know that the rabbi was head of the rabbinical court in Nemirov for sixteen years before the flight, besides the years he was in Rashkov, some years after this story.

**Summary:** The Baal Shem Tov taught the rabbi of Rashkov that every time he wanted to travel to the Land of Israel, it was a sign that there was judgment on the town, and that he must pray for it. Once, on the way to the Baal Shem Tov, the rabbi had to spend the Sabbath in Shargorod, the town that had driven him out, because of a rain that would not stop, and he prayed for it the whole Sabbath. The Baal Shem Tov laughed, and told him that had the Mokhiach not revealed the proclamation, he would have canceled the decree completely, and now the town would only burn slowly, street after street. And so it happened, and the rabbi's enemies were killed and died as had been decreed, more than twenty years later.

## 24 — The Psalms in Nemirov, and the Tatars

I will write a little of the praises of the rabbi, when he was head of the rabbinical court in Nemirov, one year after the flight. My custom was that when I put on Rabbeinu Tam tefillin, I was afraid to put them on in front of people, for I was still young. So I used to do this: during "Ashrei" and "Uva Letziyon" I went to the rabbi's big house, for no one was there at prayer time. (They prayed in the room next to the small house, and only the rabbi's attendant was in the house, and sometimes the attendant went to the market to buy things for the house.) I went into the house, put on the Rabbeinu Tam tefillin there, and lingered. Meanwhile they finished the prayer, and the rabbi came into his house, looked this way and that, and saw that no one was there. I asked him: "Our master, what are you looking for?" He said to me: "You are walking around in tefillin?" And at once I took off the head tefillin. He said to me: "Go to the study house, and see whether they have finished the prayer." I went, and they were still saying "Ashrei" and "Uva Letziyon." I came back to his house, and did not find him, for he had taken off his tefillin and gone to relieve himself, and I waited for him. When he came home and asked me, I said that they had finished the prayer, for I guessed that surely they had finished by now. He began to put on tefillin (Rabbeinu Tam tefillin).

I knew he would go to the study house, and I went there ahead of him. I found a man there standing before the ark and saying Psalms as if in his sleep. I knew the rabbi would be angry about this, for he used to say: "The covenant of the flesh (circumcision) corresponds to the covenant of the tongue. Just as with a dead organ one cannot beget children, so too with the tongue one cannot beget anything with dead speech." And the rabbi had ordered Psalms to be said in all the synagogues: the verses of "Happy are those whose way is blameless" (Psalm 119) by the letters of the name "Nemirov" and of the words "Kera Satan" (tear up the Satan), interwoven with the four-letter name of God; and before that to say Psalm 83, and to say a "May it be Your will" prayer every day after the prayer. When the rabbi came to the study house and heard the saying of the Psalms, he grew angry and said: "I have no more strength to bear you! Each one thinks only of himself. I tell you: do not take the matter of the Tatars lightly!" From there he went to the synagogue, and there his anger calmed down, and he hinted at the matter by way of a plain explanation of the text. And had I not heard his words in the study house before, I would not have understood his hints.

That day he gathered the schoolchildren into the synagogue, and commanded them to say the psalm "May the pleasantness of the Lord" ninety-one times. Before the afternoon prayer they heard that the night before a great camp of Tatars had passed half a parsah from Tulchin, and they were close to the town of Nemirov. And thank God, the Turks sent an officer after them, a pasha, and they all turned back.

**Summary:** The author, who was young then, secretly put on Rabbeinu Tam tefillin in the house of the rabbi of Nemirov, and when he went to the study house ahead of the rabbi, he found there a man saying Psalms without life. The rabbi, who had ordered Psalms to be said in all the synagogues, was angry with the congregation for not taking the danger from the Tatars seriously, and gathered the children to say "May the pleasantness" ninety-one times. That same day it became known that a great camp of Tatars had passed close to Nemirov, and a Turkish pasha turned them back.

## 25 — The Rabbi's Customs

I heard from a man named Rabbi David, who lived in Shargorod at that time: one day before that, the rabbi of Shargorod, Rabbi Mordechai, ordered the community to send a special messenger to the rabbi of Nemirov, so that he would pray about the matter of the Tatars. They sent a messenger, and the rabbi ordered them that the messenger should leave early in the morning. And the rabbi said that there was no need for a messenger, for the rabbi of Nemirov already knew. May God save His people Israel from every trouble and distress, amen.

And I will write a little of the rabbi's customs: he studied every day in tallit and tefillin. Before eating he studied a lesson of seven pages of Gemara, and even while eating, between one course and the next, he did not stop studying. After eating he slept very little, got up from his sleep, put on tefillin, and studied until the afternoon prayer. At night too he studied a great deal. Not one night passed over him on which he did not get up at exactly midnight, summer and winter, on the Sabbath and on festivals. He did a very great deal of ransoming of captives and of charity. But in his home there was a hard and pressed living. Once his son complained to him about the hardship, and the rabbi answered him: "It is written in Midrash Shmuel: 'Let the members of your household be poor.'" His study, his prayer and every holy thing he did with all his strength, until his flesh trembled on him.

**Summary:** When the Tatars threatened, the rabbi of Shargorod ordered that a messenger be sent to the rabbi of Nemirov so that he would pray, and it was said that there was no need, for the rabbi already knew. The author describes the customs of the rabbi of Nemirov: he studied all day in tallit and tefillin, got up every night at midnight, gave much charity, and lived in poverty at home. Every holy thing he did with such strength that his flesh trembled.

## 26 — Rabbi Gershon at Sea

I heard from my father-in-law that the pious Rabbi Gershon immersed himself in the sea while the ship was sailing. I asked my father-in-law: "How was he permitted to put himself in such great danger?" And he had no answer. When Rabbi Gedaliah came to us, I remembered and asked him about it. He answered me: "Once I sat with the pious Rabbi Tzvi of Kamenets on the bench in front of the house, and I asked him about this matter. He answered me: 'On this bench I sat with Rabbi Gershon, and I asked him this: I heard that you immersed yourself in the sea while the ship was sailing. How were you permitted to put yourself in such danger? And he answered laughing: What of it? All the sailors bathe in the sea, for there is a sack of leather, with a ladder of leather in it, that hangs from the ship, and they go up and down on it and bathe inside it.'"

"But I will tell you," said Rabbi Gershon, "I did not bathe in that sack. This is how it happened: I saw that there were judgments on the ship, and I wanted to immerse myself to cancel the judgments. As it happened, they came to an island and stopped the ship, and all the people of the ship went out for a walk. I said: now is the right time to immerse. I took off my clothes in the bottom of the ship, jumped into the water, and sat in the water. Meanwhile the ship moved. When I came up out of the water and saw that the ship had sailed, I said in my heart: if I go to the island, what will I do? I am naked, and I will not be able to say anything holy. And besides, I have nothing there to eat and drink. I said: I will chase after the ship and catch up with it. I was sure of myself that I knew how to swim well, for once in the river Dniester I saw a Jew drowning, about half a parsah away from me, and I saved him.

"And so I did: I swam after the ship and reached it. But since it is high and smooth, coated with tar, I had nothing to hold on to. I shouted, and my voice could not be heard because of the noise of the ship, and again the ship moved away from me. I swam with all my strength, and my hands slipped. I did this several times, until I was very frightened, and I began to sink in the water, going down and coming up, and I could not say a single word, not even the confession. And I thought in my heart: both to drown, and to lose the World to Come, for this is almost like someone who takes his own life! And at once someone came out of the ship and saw me. He pulled me out, put me in a small boat of his, brought me to the big ship, and threw me into it. I went down to the stern of the ship, and lay there about two hours, and vomited a great deal of all the water I had swallowed. I lay there until I had rested a little, and put on my clothes. I said: I will pay the man for the kindness he did me, so that he will not inherit my World to Come because of it. I took a purse of money, and searched for him all over the ship, and there was no such Ishmaelite there. And I said: from this it follows that it was 'that Arab' (as in the stories of the Gemara in which Elijah the Prophet appears in the form of an Arab)."

**Summary:** Rabbi Gershon told that he immersed himself in the sea because he saw judgments on the ship, but the ship sailed while he was in the water. He swam after it and could not get a hold on it, and he began to drown. A man pulled him out and brought him back to the ship, but when Rabbi Gershon looked for him to pay him, he did not find him anywhere on the ship, and he said that it must have been Elijah the Prophet in the form of an Arab.

## 27 — The Mark the Baal Shem Tov Saw on Rabbi Gershon

I heard a story from the rabbi of our community: the Baal Shem Tov saw on his brother-in-law, Rabbi Gershon, the mark of adultery. He was very grieved by it, and was ashamed to tell him, and wondered at it. Even so, he told him. Rabbi Gershon answered him: "You are a liar!" and struck the Baal Shem Tov. The Baal Shem Tov said to him: "Wait for me until tomorrow, for even so I see on you the mark of adultery."

At night, in the unifications he performed, he saw the matter, and the next day he said to him: "You took upon yourself to separate from your wife, and I heard in the heavens that Maimonides ruled that this is as if he had said to his wife 'You are to me like my mother.' Although it is not a complete vow, even so the mark of the nocturnal emission, for which you fasted from Sabbath to Sabbath, was stirred up against you. And since you broke the fence of separation that you took upon yourself, the mark grew stronger, and I see it on you like adultery." And Rabbi Gershon admitted to him that it was true. He had broken the separation because he wanted to travel to the Holy Land, and he could not win his wife over in this matter, for he was far from her. So he broke the separation. The two of them worked at the repair, until they had repaired the matter. And from that union the preacher of Ladyzhin was born, of whom the Mokhiach of Polonne said that he had never had a nocturnal emission. <!-- MATURE: sexual content (a mark of adultery, separation from a wife, nocturnal emission) -->

**Summary:** The Baal Shem Tov saw on Rabbi Gershon the sign of a grave sin, and Rabbi Gershon grew angry, called him a liar and struck him. The next day the Baal Shem Tov explained to him that the sign came from his having taken upon himself to separate from his wife and then having broken it, and Rabbi Gershon admitted it and made a repair. From that time his son was born, who was later the preacher of Ladyzhin.

## 28 — The Fast for Rain in Jerusalem

I heard from that preacher: once Rabbi Shalom, who had come from the Land of the West (Morocco), decreed a fast for rain in Jerusalem. He was a great and important tzaddik, and he ordered that everyone gather in one synagogue to pray together, and he sent for Rabbi Gershon to lead the prayers before the ark. In Jerusalem the custom is that whoever leads the prayers before the ark must speak words of moral reproof. And the custom of the preachers there is to quote the Gemara, the Midrash or the verses exactly as they are, and if one changes the wording, it is a disgrace. Rabbi Gershon prepared to preach, and went over the wording many times, so as to say the words exactly. He did not sleep all night, and before morning he grew hoarse.

He said to his son, Rabbi Leib: "My voice is spoiled. Go to Rabbi Shalom and tell him that I cannot lead the prayers." And so the congregation broke up, and each one prayed in the place he was used to, and Rabbi Shalom prayed in his own study house, in his place, and someone else went down before the ark. In the middle of the Song at the Sea, Rabbi Shalom heard that Rabbi Gershon's voice had come back, and right after the silent prayer he ordered the cantor to step down from the prayer stand, and Rabbi Gershon to go up to the prayer stand to pray the repetition aloud and say the Selichot. Rabbi Gershon went down before the ark, and after the repetition he began to say Selichot. He said the opening, and then he left the ark, and did not want to say any more Selichot. His son, Rabbi Leib, asked him: "Why did you do that?" He answered him: "In the opening the prayer flowed fluently from my mouth, and I felt that if I said more Selichot, rain would fall at once, and I was afraid of pride. So I did not go on praying. For I know that rain will fall for two or three days in a row after this." And so it was.

**Summary:** At a fast for rain in Jerusalem Rabbi Shalom asked Rabbi Gershon to lead the prayers before the ark, but Rabbi Gershon prepared all night for the sermon and grew hoarse. When his voice came back, he went down before the ark, but after the opening of the Selichot he stopped, for he felt that if he went on, rain would fall at once, and he was afraid he would become proud. A few days later rain fell for two or three days in a row.

## 29 — Rabbi Gershon's Purse

I also heard from Rabbi Pinchas the teacher, who had earlier been a preacher (and it seems to me that the story took place in the city of Safed): there was a rabbi of the city there, whom in their language they call "Chacham." He was a very good man, and he loved Rabbi Gershon. Once Rabbi Gershon said to the Chacham: "My money has run out, and I have had to borrow for the Sabbath expenses." One day it was very cold, and Rabbi Gershon put on a vest padded with cotton, and went to the study house to pray. After the prayer they stayed in the study house, and each one studied his lesson. Rabbi Gershon walked back and forth in the study house, and took snuff. And from the vest there fell a small purse of the kind that locks, and in the purse were five small coins called *sechsers*. In the Holy Land they did not know such a purse.

The Chacham saw that the purse had fallen from Rabbi Gershon, picked it up, and felt it from the outside, for he could not open it. He felt that there were five coins inside, and thought they were five zlotys. The Chacham was astonished at Rabbi Gershon, that he had let a lie come out of his mouth, and there they are very strict about lying. He began to speak with Rabbi Gershon: "Your honor borrowed money for the Sabbath expenses, because your honor had no money?" He answered him: "That is right." The Chacham said to him: "Your honor is telling a lie!" And he said: "I spoke the truth!" And so they went over the argument again, this one saying this and that one saying that. Meanwhile the Chacham rushed to the holy ark, and swore that Rabbi Gershon was telling a lie. And Rabbi Gershon sprang up and put him under a ban.

The Chacham took off his shoes (as is the custom of one under a ban), went home, gathered the whole congregation, and told them what had happened. He showed them the purse, and they too felt the purse and said that there were five zlotys in it. And the Chacham complained before them: "It is not enough that he is a liar, he also put me under a ban!" The people of the congregation went to Rabbi Gershon and asked him: "Why did you put our Chacham under a ban?" He said to them: "Because he swore a false oath." They showed him the purse and said: "Look, there are five zlotys here!" Rabbi Gershon said: "Why did he not open the purse before he swore?" They answered him: "He could not open it." He said to them: "He should have torn the purse open and looked. And this coin does not pass among merchants here, and it is just silver, like the silver spice box and the other silver vessels I have." The Chacham made peace with Rabbi Gershon, and Rabbi Gershon released him from the ban.

Afterward a letter came from the Baal Shem Tov to Rabbi Gershon, and he wrote in it: "I saw that they were judging you in a heavenly palace and wanted to sentence you to death, Heaven forbid, for having been insolent toward the rabbi, the head of the rabbinical court. I wanted to go to the palace to plead for you, and they shut the palace before me and I could not go in. I said: Master of the world, he did it for the sake of Your name. And a voice went out: 'Leave him, for he was zealous for the Lord of Hosts.' Even so, they ruled that he would be blind for one month, and a blind man is counted as dead. And so, from today on, do not be so harsh." Rabbi Gershon wrote to the Baal Shem Tov: "I do not know what to think of you. The story is true, but it amazes me that you saw them judging me even before the thing happened." (For in the Baal Shem Tov's letter it was written on what day it was written.)

And something like this I heard from the rabbi of our community: Rabbi Lipman of Chmielnik quarreled with the rabbi of his town over a question of Jewish law; one forbade and the other permitted. When Rabbi Lipman was with the Baal Shem Tov on Rosh Hashanah, the Baal Shem Tov said during the meal: "Today I saw them sentencing a certain scholar to death, for having been insolent toward the head of the rabbinical court of his community." Rabbi Lipman understood that he was speaking about him, and began to weep. The Baal Shem Tov said to him: "Do not be afraid, for I prayed for you, and they released you from the sentence."

**Summary:** In Safed the Chacham, the rabbi of the city, saw a purse with five coins fall from Rabbi Gershon, and swore that Rabbi Gershon had lied when he said he had no money for the Sabbath, and Rabbi Gershon put him under a ban. It turned out that the coins were not in use in the Land of Israel and so did not count as money, and the two made peace. The Baal Shem Tov wrote to Rabbi Gershon that he had seen in Heaven that they were judging him for having been insolent toward the rabbi, and that they had ruled that he would be blind for one month, and he warned him not to be so harsh. Rabbi Lipman too, who had quarreled with the rabbi of his town, heard from the Baal Shem Tov that he had been judged in Heaven, and that the Baal Shem Tov had prayed for him and they had released him.

## 30 — The Song About Rabbi Gershon's Wife

I also heard from Rabbi Pinchas: when Rabbi Gershon was in the Holy Land, there was a custom there from ancient times to strain the water, both for cooking and for drinking, for their water is collected in cisterns, and there are many worms and gnats in it. Rabbi Gershon's wife did not know about this, and did not strain the water, for in her country there was no custom of straining water. The women made up a mocking song about her, and mocked her in the synagogue. She came home weeping bitterly: "Why did you bring me to a distant land, where they mock me?" Rabbi Gershon comforted her and said: "Show me the woman who made up this song." She showed him the woman as she was coming out of the synagogue. Rabbi Gershon spat and said in astonishment: "What? This woman can speak?" And the woman became mute.

**Summary:** In the Land of Israel they used to strain the cistern water of worms, and Rabbi Gershon's wife, who did not know the custom, did not strain it, and the women made up a mocking song about her. She wept before her husband, and he asked her to show him the woman who had made up the song. When he saw her, he spat and asked in astonishment whether she could speak, and the woman became mute.

## 31 — The Sick Girl in Zaslav

I heard from my son, Rabbi Leib: once Rabbi Hirsch, the son-in-law of the great Rabbi David, head of the rabbinical court of Ostroh, spent the Sabbath with us. (And Rabbi Isaac was the brother-in-law of Rabbi Hirtz, the son of this rabbi.) At the end of the Sabbath his son told a story he had heard from him: Rabbi Isaac, the rabbi's son, had a young daughter, thirteen years old, who was already married, and she fell ill. When the Baal Shem Tov was in the town of Zaslav, they showed him the sick girl, and he ordered that the next day they call a woman healer for her, to put leeches on her, and he said to open the *meridin* for her. The rich Rabbi Isaac was afraid to rely on the Baal Shem Tov, and asked the duchess's doctor in the town. The doctor said that *meridin* happens even in a young girl. Rabbi Isaac came home, and sent for the woman healer.

The Baal Shem Tov's lodging was with Rabbi David, who had earlier been head of the rabbinical court in Ostroh. Rabbi Hirsch, the rabbi's son-in-law, walked with the Baal Shem Tov to Rabbi Isaac's house, through the passageway of the house. Some horses were standing there, and the Baal Shem Tov went from horse to horse, looked at them, patted them, and one horse pleased him. He said to Rabbi Hirsch: "Perhaps he might sell me this horse?" Rabbi Hirsch answered that he did not know. They went into the house, and the Baal Shem Tov said to Rabbi Isaac: "Sell me this horse." He answered him: "Our master, it is written 'You shall not covet.'" He said to him: "Fool! And even so, I covet." And Rabbi Isaac did not want to sell. Afterward the Baal Shem Tov saw the woman healer, and said: "Is there no other in the town, that you called precisely this devourer?" This woman was an important person in the town. She opened the *meridin* for the sick girl, and even so the girl did not get well.

The Baal Shem Tov went home, and Rabbi Isaac traveled with his daughter to a Tatar healer in the town of Konstantin. The Baal Shem Tov heard about it, and traveled to Konstantin himself, and was angry with him and said: "You too do this to me?" Rabbi Isaac promised him that he would go home, but that was not his intention. Only when Rabbi Isaac saw that the Tatar healer was no help at all did he travel home with his daughter.

Once, close to the Sabbath, the girl grew very weak. Rabbi David the rabbi made a great stir, gathered teachers to say Psalms, and blessed her with another name (they changed her name). He sent a letter by the hand of a gentile to Mezhibuzh, by messenger, so that he could go on the Sabbath to the Baal Shem Tov, for him to pray for her. The gentile arrived on the Sabbath, after the Torah reading, before the Musaf prayer. At the end of the Sabbath the Baal Shem Tov ordered a letter to be written and given into the gentile's hand, and in it was written: "I received your letter before Musaf, and I prayed a great prayer for her, and I was not answered, not a thing and not half a thing, and I do not know what to think of it. Unless you blessed her with another name. Let me know the name at once."

In the end it turned out that the girl was pregnant, and when she gave birth, she got well. Afterward, when the Baal Shem Tov was in Zaslav again, they said to him: "You recognized the woman healer, and you also knew that the sick girl had a new name. Why did you not recognize that she was pregnant?" He answered them: "Do you think she was really pregnant? From my prayer and from the prayer of your father she became pregnant." (And the letter the Baal Shem Tov sent was in the handwriting of Rabbi Yaakov of Yampol.)

**Summary:** The granddaughter of Rabbi David of Ostroh fell ill, and her family did not fully rely on the Baal Shem Tov: they called a woman healer he did not like, and also took her to a Tatar healer, and she did not get well. When her condition grew worse, the Baal Shem Tov prayed for her and was not answered, and he asked whether they had changed her name. In the end it turned out that she was pregnant, and after the birth she got well, and the Baal Shem Tov said that she had become pregnant through his prayer and her grandfather's prayer.

## 32 — The False Accusation in Zaslav

Another story: the Baal Shem Tov was staying in the town of Zaslav, and spent several Sabbaths there. Meanwhile a dead gentile was found in a field, and brought into town. The gentiles wanted to make a false accusation against the Jews, and went to the duchess and told her that the Jews had killed him. The duchess was afraid for herself, for her husband, the duke, had died a strange death on the road, because he had killed holy Jews on false accusations. So she said: "I do not want to judge a capital case, unless the matter is investigated and it becomes perfectly clear that the Jews killed him."

When the Jews in the town heard of this, trembling seized them, and they sent Rabbi Isaac to the Baal Shem Tov's lodging to tell him. The Baal Shem Tov was standing with one foot on the bench and one foot on the ground, and Rabbi Tzvi the scribe was saying the Zohar before him, and the Baal Shem Tov was repeating after him with great fervor, his face burning. When Rabbi Isaac came to the threshold of the house, the Baal Shem Tov signaled to him with his hand to go, and he went. The fear grew and grew, and they sent him a second time, and again the Baal Shem Tov signaled to him to go. Finally they urged Rabbi David the rabbi to go to the Baal Shem Tov himself, for surely he would not send him away with a "go and come back," since he was old and respected. Rabbi David went to the Baal Shem Tov, and the Baal Shem Tov said to him: "I do not hear them talking about it at the court (of the duchess) at all."

Once, after the Sabbath, in the middle of the day, after the meal, the Baal Shem Tov lay on a chest leaning on his hand, and people stood before him. He bent his ear, like someone who wants to listen, and said: "Now they are talking at the court about this accusation." And he said: "Heat the mikveh for me quickly." He went to the mikveh, came back, and said: "Do not be afraid, for there is no danger at all." The community sent the intercessors (the men who spoke for the Jews with the authorities) to the court, and they heard that the gentiles had complained before the duchess about the murdered gentile, and she had ordered her doctor to examine the dead man, and find out whether he had died of himself or not. The community was afraid to rely on the Baal Shem Tov, and they gave the doctor thirty zlotys. And thank God, no accusation at all came of it.

The head of the rabbinical court of Zaslav was Rabbi David's brother-in-law. They asked the Baal Shem Tov: "Why did you not tell us in advance, before the dead man was found, so that we would not be afraid when he was found in the field?" He answered them: "I prayed in the mikveh about this very thing: why was the matter hidden from me in advance? And they answered me that it was a punishment for me, for having been lazy about the eulogy of a Torah scholar, who was called Rabbi Chaim of Brody." And he also said to the rabbi of Zaslav: "Do you think I do not know that you hid several times? Once in the bathhouse, under the benches, and in other places." And the rabbi admitted all of them to him.

**Summary:** In Zaslav a dead gentile was found in a field, and the gentiles wanted to falsely accuse the Jews of murdering him. The Baal Shem Tov, deep in Torah, sent the messengers away again and again, and afterward heard from afar what was being said at the duchess's court, immersed in the mikveh, and said there was no danger, and indeed no accusation came of it. He explained that he had not known about the matter in advance as a punishment for having been lazy about the eulogy of a Torah scholar, and revealed to the town rabbi that he knew the rabbi had hidden several times.

## 33 — The Fire Around the Dancers

I heard when the rabbi of Nemirov came: once, on Simchat Torah, the people close to the Baal Shem Tov were rejoicing and dancing, and drank a great deal of wine from the Baal Shem Tov's cellar. The Baal Shem Tov's wife, the righteous woman, said: "They will not leave any wine for Kiddush and Havdalah." She went in to the Baal Shem Tov and said to him: "Tell them to stop drinking and dancing, for you will have no wine left for Kiddush and Havdalah." The Baal Shem Tov said to her jokingly: "You have spoken well. Go and tell them to stop and go home." When she opened the door, they were dancing in a circle, and she saw a blazing fire around them like a wedding canopy. She took the vessels, went down to the cellar herself, and brought them as much wine as they wanted. Afterward the Baal Shem Tov asked her: "Did you tell them to go?" She said to him: "You should have told them yourself."

**Summary:** On Simchat Torah the Baal Shem Tov's disciples drank a great deal of wine, and his wife was worried that no wine would be left for Kiddush and Havdalah. The Baal Shem Tov sent her to tell them to go home, but when she opened the door she saw a blazing fire around the dancers like a wedding canopy. She herself brought them more wine from the cellar, and told her husband that he should have told them himself.

## 34 — How the Maggid of Mezritch Drew Close

I heard from the rabbi of Drazhnitz, who was head of the rabbinical court in Pavoloch, the story of how the great Maggid drew close to the Baal Shem Tov. The Maggid fasted seven or eight times from Sabbath to Sabbath without a break, and became very ill. Once Rabbi Mendel of Bar came to the town of Torchin, and stayed with the parnas of the month. The Maggid lived in a small house next to the parnas's house, which was called the "lower room." Rabbi Mendel went into his host's passageway, and heard the words of the Maggid, who was studying with a pupil, and his words pleased him. He went in to him, and saw that he was very ill. He said to him: "Have you not heard that there is a Baal Shem Tov in the world? Let your honor travel to him, and he will heal him." The Maggid answered: "It is better to take refuge in the Lord than to trust in man." When Rabbi Mendel came to the Baal Shem Tov, he spoke in praise of the Maggid and said: "I was in the town of Torchin, and I saw a fine vessel." The Baal Shem Tov said: "I already saw him some years ago, and I long for him to come to me." There are several versions of how the Maggid came to the Baal Shem Tov, but I heard that his relatives pressed him to travel to the Baal Shem Tov.

When the Maggid came to the Baal Shem Tov, he found him sitting on his bed and studying. He greeted him, and asked him to heal him. The Baal Shem Tov rebuked him and said: "My horses do not eat matzah." From great weakness sweat came over the Maggid, and he went outside and sat on the bench in front of the house to rest. He saw a young man, called him over, and said to him: "Please, go to the Baal Shem Tov and say to him: why does he not keep the verse 'You shall love the stranger'?" The young man was Rabbi Yaakov of Anipoli. He took pity on the Maggid, and went in to the Baal Shem Tov, but was afraid to tell him. He acted wisely: he walked to the far end of the house, and turned back to go out and run away, and on his way out he said: "A sad man (a melancholy man) is sitting in front of the house, and asked me to say to your honor: why have you not kept 'You shall love the stranger'?" And he left the house. At once the Baal Shem Tov gathered ten men, went out to the Maggid and made peace with him, and wanted to heal him with words.

I heard from Rabbi Gershon of Pavoloch that the Baal Shem Tov went to him for about two weeks, sat opposite him and said Psalms. Afterward the Baal Shem Tov said to him: "I wanted to heal you with words, for that is a lasting cure. But now I have to treat you with medicines." He gave him lodgings, and twelve zlotys every week for his expenses. Rabbi Yaakov and Rabbi Eliyahu went to visit him all the time, and sometimes debated with him over the Gemara and Tosafot. The Maggid could not go to the Baal Shem Tov from great weakness. After a short time he began to recover, and went to the Baal Shem Tov, to his table.

Once the Maggid fainted, and they tried to revive him until midday. The Baal Shem Tov went to the mikveh three times, and sent to a nobleman three parsahs away, and bought from him a precious stone called a diamond, for thirty zlotys. They ground it up, and gave it to him to drink. Afterward Rabbi Yaakov and his companions came to visit him, and asked him why he had fainted, and he did not answer them. They asked the people of the house whether he had gone out in the night, and they said: "He went out, and stayed outside, and when he came back home he began to faint." They asked him: "Where were you?"

He said to them: "The Baal Shem Tov sent his attendant for me at midnight. I came, and found him sitting, with a small cushion on his head, dressed in a wolfskin turned inside out. He asked me: 'Have you studied the wisdom of Kabbalah?' I said: 'Yes.' A book lay before him on the table, and he commanded me to read before him from this book. The book was written in short passages, and every passage began with the words: 'Rabbi Yishmael said: Metatron, the Prince of the Countenance, told me.' I read before him a page or half a page. The Baal Shem Tov said: 'That is not how it is. I will say it before you.' And he said it before me, and in the middle of it he trembled, stood up and said: 'We are engaged in the Work of the Chariot, and I am sitting?' And he said the words standing. In the middle of the words he laid me down on the bed like a circle, and I did not see him anymore; I only heard thunderings, and saw terrible lightnings and torches. So it went for about two hours. I was very frightened, and from this fear I began to faint."

And I, the author, say: it seems to me that this was the giving of the Torah. For I heard from the pious Rabbi of Polonne that he received from the Baal Shem Tov the Torah belonging to his soul, with thunderings and lightnings. And he would also say the expression "with all kinds of K.Z.," as is written in the holy Zohar. I have not seen in the Zohar that "with all kinds of K.Z." is mentioned at the giving of the Torah. But I heard this expression from the rabbi: "Just as the Jewish people received the Torah as a whole, so the Baal Shem Tov received it in particular."

And afterward, once, the people close to the Baal Shem Tov asked about a passage in the Zohar, and he explained it to them. They asked the Maggid again, and his explanation was exactly as the Baal Shem Tov had said. They told the Baal Shem Tov, and he said: "You are like bees; why, he knows the Torah himself." When the Maggid left the Baal Shem Tov, the Baal Shem Tov blessed him, and afterward the Baal Shem Tov bowed his head, so that the Maggid would bless him. The Maggid did not want to, and the Baal Shem Tov took his hand, put it on his head, and the Maggid blessed him.

**Summary:** The Maggid of Mezritch became very ill from much fasting, and under pressure from his relatives traveled to the Baal Shem Tov, but the Baal Shem Tov rebuked him, and the Maggid sent him through someone else the question "Why do you not keep 'You shall love the stranger'?" The Baal Shem Tov made peace with him and healed him with Psalms, with medicines and with a ground diamond. One night at midnight the Baal Shem Tov taught him the Work of the Chariot with thunderings and lightnings, and the author thinks this was the giving of the Torah. When they parted, the Baal Shem Tov blessed the Maggid, and insisted that the Maggid bless him too.

## 35 — Rabbi Eliezer of Amsterdam, and the Unfit Book

I heard from Rabbi Yoel, the preacher of Nemirov, who was always with the Rabbi of Polonne in Rashkov and in Shargorod. In Nemirov I heard from him: once the Baal Shem Tov spent the Sabbath in Shargorod. At the end of the Sabbath, after Havdalah, his custom was to tell what he had seen on the Sabbath. He said that he had seen that Rabbi Eliezer, the rabbi of Amsterdam, had died, and that he had died on the previous Sabbath, which was the Sabbath of the portion of Bereishit. "I asked him: what was the reason that he died? And he said: two men from Poland shamed him on Simchat Torah." (Rabbi Yoel did not want to give their names.) "They said this to him: 'Here you are a prince, and in the land of Poland you would not have been fit to be rabbi even of the tailors.' And he accepted death upon himself, but he said: 'I will take revenge on them with plague and with the sword.' I asked him: 'What will you do about your wife, the rabbanit?' And he said: 'I will take her away from them.'" And I forgot to ask him whether he meant alive, or whether he thought that she too would die.

That year the son of the Rabbi of Polonne arrived. His father asked him: "How is Rabbi Eliezer the rabbi?" He said: "He has already passed away." He asked him: "Did he pass away on the Sabbath of Bereishit?" He said: "Yes." And the son asked: "How do you know this? No one got here before me." His father said to him: "We knew at once, on the Sabbath of the portion of Noah, for the Baal Shem Tov told us." And the son was greatly amazed. The father asked him about the rabbi's wife, and the son said that Rabbi Avraham Shverdlik had come from Jerusalem and taken her to Jerusalem. The father asked about the men who had quarreled with the rabbi, and the son said that they had been weakened by plague and had been driven out into the fields, and Ishmaelites had come and killed them.

The Baal Shem Tov also said on that very Sabbath that he had seen that a new book would be printed that year. He did not know its name, for it had not yet been given a name. This book was written by one of the sect of Shabbetai Tzvi, may his name be blotted out. "The lips of a strange woman drip honey": its language is beautiful, and people will desire it, and it will defile the world, Heaven forbid. Afterward, when the book came out, the Rabbi of Polonne bought it, for he had not known its name before. When the Baal Shem Tov came to him, and the book was lying on the table, as soon as the Baal Shem Tov opened the door he said: "An unfit book is lying on your table!" The rabbi asked: "Which one?" And the Baal Shem Tov pointed at it with his finger, and it was the book *Chemdat HaYamim*. They took a rag from the corner, wrapped the book in it, and put it under the table.

And Rabbi Yoel told me: once the rabbi saw in a dream that one of the men of his minyan had converted. Trembling seized him, and he hurried and sent to wake all the men of the minyan, and saw that all of them, thank God, were well. He asked them: "Perhaps one of you committed a sin in secret, Heaven forbid?" And he told them the dream. A righteous man, Rabbi Eliezer of Tomashpol, answered: "I know of nothing, except that on that night, before going to sleep, I looked into the book *Chemdat HaYamim*."

And Rabbi Yoel also told me that Shabbetai Tzvi came to the Baal Shem Tov to ask him for a *tikkun*. Rabbi Yoel said this: the *tikkun* is to bind oneself soul to soul, spirit to spirit, inner soul to inner soul. The Baal Shem Tov began to bind himself to him cautiously, for he was afraid, since Shabbetai Tzvi was very wicked. Once the Baal Shem Tov was asleep, and Shabbetai Tzvi, may his name be blotted out, came and tried to lead him astray, Heaven forbid. And the Baal Shem Tov threw him with a great throw, until he fell to the lowest depths of the netherworld. The Baal Shem Tov looked at the place where he was, and saw that he was with Jesus on one board. And Rabbi Yoel told that the Baal Shem Tov said that there was a holy spark in Shabbetai Tzvi, and the Satan caught it in his net. And the Baal Shem Tov heard the story of how his fall came about, through pride and anger. And I am too lazy to write it; perhaps I will write it, so that people will know how deep pride goes.

**Summary:** At the end of the Sabbath the Baal Shem Tov told that Rabbi Eliezer, the rabbi of Amsterdam, had died on the previous Sabbath, after two men from Poland shamed him, and months later the news arrived and confirmed his words. The Baal Shem Tov also foresaw that a beautifully written book by one of the sect of Shabbetai Tzvi would be printed, and when he found it on the table of the Rabbi of Polonne, he called it an unfit book. At the end it is told that Shabbetai Tzvi came to the Baal Shem Tov to ask for a *tikkun*, and tried to lead him astray, and the Baal Shem Tov threw him down to the lowest depths.

## 36 — The Preacher of Drohobych in Horochov

This story too I heard from Rabbi Yoel: Rabbi Yitzchak of Drohobych was taken on as preacher in the great town of Horochov, and stayed there several weeks. This happened: a butcher was left owing money to the holder of the *taksa* (the man who leased the tax on meat). The holder of the taksa was not at home, and his wife took pledges from the butcher, even pillows and blankets. The butcher's wife came to Rabbi Yitzchak with a great complaint and great weeping. Rabbi Yitzchak sent for the taksa holder's wife, and ordered her to give back the pledges, and she did not listen to him. Rabbi Yitzchak cursed her, and at once a baby of hers died. When her husband came back from his journey, she told him the story, and said: "We appointed a preacher, and he cursed me, and at once the child died."

Rabbi Yitzchak went home to the town of Ostroh, for he was preacher there. The community of Horochov sent wagons after him, so that he would come to Horochov with his whole household. What did the taksa holder do? He sent a special messenger with a letter, and wrote in it: "If he has not yet moved from his place, let him stay in his place. And if he is already on the way, let him go back to his place. For even if he comes, he will be forced to leave here." Rabbi Yitzchak wrote him an answer: "I will come to the town when they carry your bier toward me." And so it was: when Rabbi Yitzchak came in through the town gate, they were carrying the taksa holder on his bier through the town gate, and they could not get out with the dead man, and they had to move the wagon to one side, and only then did they go out with the dead man.

The taksa holder had a large family, and there was a hatred pent up in their hearts, for they were afraid of the preacher. "And this strife is like a breach of water," which grows wider and wider, until the dispute grew strong, and whoever harmed the preacher did not live. Even the head of the rabbinical court there, Rabbi Itzik, who later became head of the rabbinical court in Brody, and afterward was taken to be head of the rabbinical court in Hamburg, was one of the people in the dispute. But since Rabbi Yechezkel, head of the rabbinical court of Tomashpol, was a relative of this rabbi, he wrote to him, for his honor's sake, that he should make peace with the preacher, "for he is a consuming fire." And the rabbi made peace with him.

Once there was a great thunderbolt, and it struck the synagogue during the afternoon prayer, and killed two men: one by the eastern wall and one in the vestibule. The people of the town came and said to the preacher: "Our master, you have killed the people of the Lord!" He answered and said: "The one by the eastern wall was killed because of strife. And the one in the vestibule was killed because of a false oath he swore in Brody over three zlotys, and he was holding them in his hand during the prayer and playing with them." And they found the three zlotys in his hand.

**Summary:** Rabbi Yitzchak of Drohobych, the preacher of Horochov, cursed a woman who had taken a poor butcher's pillows and blankets as a pledge, and at once her baby died. Her husband wrote to the preacher not to come back to the town, and the preacher answered that he would come when they carried the husband on his bier, and so it happened, exactly at the town gate. From then on, whoever harmed the preacher did not live, and when lightning killed two men in the synagogue, the preacher explained that one was punished for strife and the other for a false oath, and they found the zlotys in his hand.

## 37 — The Oath Before Rabbi Yitzchak

I heard from the rabbi of our community: two men with a lawsuit came before Rabbi Yitzchak for judgment. Rabbi Yitzchak ruled that one of them had to swear an oath, and he took it upon himself to swear. The other side shouted that the ruling should be carried out, for he was very angry, because he knew for certain that the other side was claiming a lie. Rabbi Yitzchak said: "Why are you in a hurry for him to carry out the ruling? This is my way: whoever claims a lie before me, one of his descendants will die. Perhaps he will regret it, and admit it, and live." And so it was: one of his descendants died, and he regretted it and admitted the truth.

**Summary:** A man who had to swear an oath in a lawsuit before Rabbi Yitzchak agreed to swear, and the other side, who knew he was lying, demanded that he swear at once. Rabbi Yitzchak said that for whoever lies before him one of his descendants dies, and so he was waiting; perhaps he would regret it. And so it was: one of his descendants died, and the man admitted the truth.

## 38 — The Oxen, and the Stolen Bridle

I heard from the rabbi of our community: Rabbi Yonah of Kaminka, the brother of Rabbi Baruch, sent oxen to be sold in the villages, at a time when there was a war with the Greeks. They heard that the Greeks had robbed Rabbi Yonah's oxen. Rabbi Baruch too had sent oxen on the road, and he was afraid that his oxen would be robbed as well. They sent Rabbi Yosef of Kaminka to the Baal Shem Tov, and found him washing his hands for the meal. The Baal Shem Tov told Rabbi Yosef to wash his hands too, and after the blessing over the bread he said: "Why did they not speak with me when they set out? I would have guarded them." Afterward the Baal Shem Tov opened the Zohar, looked into it for one moment, and said: "I see Rabbi Baruch's oxen, that they are safe, and they were not robbed."

Rabbi Yosef asked the Baal Shem Tov: "And that is written in the Zohar?" He answered him: "This is what our Sages said on the verse 'And God saw the light, that it was good': that it was good to hide it away. For by the light of the six days of creation one could look from one end of the world to the other. And where did the Holy One, blessed be He, hide it? In the Torah. And what they said, 'for the righteous,' means: for the righteous who will come into the world in the future. And everyone who merits to find the hidden light in the Torah looks by it from one end of the world to the other. And you think I saw only the oxen? In that same seeing I also saw something that happened in the city of Amsterdam." And he told him the story, and I did not hear it.

A story: the Baal Shem Tov wanted to set out on a journey, and since it was the time for the blessing of the new moon, he waited until night. When he went to bless the new moon, one of the companions went with him, and wanted to look back, and the Baal Shem Tov did not let him look, for he said: "Someone is stealing the horses' bridle." (The horses were harnessed to the wagon, so as to leave right after the blessing of the new moon.) "And he is stealing because he has no money for the Sabbath expenses." After the blessing of the new moon the attendant began to shout: "Who stole the bridle?" The Baal Shem Tov said to him: "Do not shout. Go to such-and-such a man, take eighteen groschen, and redeem the bridle, for the thief pawned it there. And do not make the matter public."

**Summary:** When Rabbi Baruch was afraid that robbers would take his oxen, the Baal Shem Tov looked for a moment into the Zohar and said they were safe, and explained that the light by which one sees from one end of the world to the other is hidden in the Torah. Another time, on the way to bless the new moon, the Baal Shem Tov knew that a poor man was stealing the horses' bridle so as to have money for the Sabbath. He did not let his companion look, and afterward sent the attendant to quietly redeem the bridle from the man with whom it was pawned.

## 39 — The Drunken Shochet

It happened in the country: there was a shochet who was very frivolous and a drunkard. Once the Rabbi of Polonne called a certain man, after the morning prayer, into a room, and said to him: "I saw the Baal Shem Tov, and he went over the matter of the shochet before me several times. I cannot explain how the thing was, for he also went over the matter of the gentile butchers before me. Even so, the main thing was his strictness about the shochet, and he told me to keep an eye on him, and to catch him at something." And so it was. The rabbi was very careful not to harm a person's livelihood, and so the shochet had kept his post all this time. "And since I caught him, the rabbi ordered a rabbinical court to be convened, and testimony to be taken from the butcher, and if by Torah law it was so, they were to remove him from his post." The butcher testified about the matter, and they removed the shochet.

**Summary:** In a certain town there was a frivolous, drunken shochet, and the Rabbi of Polonne, who was very careful not to harm a person's livelihood, left him in his post. After the Baal Shem Tov appeared to the rabbi and went over the matter of the shochet before him several times, the rabbi ordered that an eye be kept on him. When they caught him, a rabbinical court sat, the butcher testified, and the shochet was removed from his post.

## 40 — Rabbi Avraham, the Maggid's Son

A story: when Rabbi Avraham, the son of the great Maggid, was taken on as preacher in the town of Fastov, the matchmaker was the famous pious Rabbi Menachem Nachum of Chernobyl. I heard from Rabbi Yoel of Pratahor, who spent the festival of Passover with Rabbi Nachum in the town of Pohrebishtch: the day after Yom Kippur a messenger came to Rabbi Nachum from Rabbi Avraham, for Rabbi Avraham had grown weak on Yom Kippur, and at the time of Ne'ilah he had stopped speaking. They asked him whether to send for Rabbi Nachum, and he nodded his head to mean "yes." They sent for him, and meanwhile Rabbi Avraham passed away. Rabbi Nachum went to the mikveh, for he said: "I will pray for him in my own place." Before the festival (Sukkot) they heard that Rabbi Avraham had passed away, but they did not tell Rabbi Nachum. On the intermediate days of the festival a man revealed it to Rabbi Nachum in all innocence, and Rabbi Nachum began to beat his head against the wall, and wept a great weeping for about two hours. They could not quiet him, until they took Rabbi Nachum in their arms, carried him to the sukkah, and said: "Rebbe, remember that today is a festival!" And then his weeping calmed down.

He began to speak in praise of Rabbi Avraham, and said: "Once I was in the town of Anipoli, and I asked him whether he sees his father (the Maggid, who had already passed away). He said: 'Yes.' I asked: 'In a dream or awake?' He answered: 'He comes to me in a dream and speaks with me, and afterward I wake from my sleep, and he speaks with me awake.'" Rabbi Avraham told Rabbi Nachum that there was a rich man there who had caused him grief several times. (Rabbi Yoel did not want to make this rich man's name public.)

"I complained to my father," said Rabbi Avraham, "and my father said to me: 'I will send for him.' I asked: 'Where will you call him to?' He said to me: 'To your study house.' And he sent for him my servant, who is called Rabbi Filet." Rabbi Filet was at that time in the region of Polesia, about sixty parsahs from Anipoli. He came to the rich man's house, knocked at the door and said: "Open for me!" The rich man recognized him by his voice, and was surprised, and said in his heart: "But he is not at home." And at once he explained it to himself: "Perhaps he came home toward evening." He greeted him with great joy, and they asked each other how they were. Afterward Rabbi Filet said: "There is no time to talk at length, for the rabbi is calling you." The rich man said to him: "What are you talking about? Our master has already died and gone to his eternal world!" Rabbi Filet said to him: "Even so, he is calling you." And the rich man went with him.

When the rich man came to Rabbi Avraham's study house, he saw the Maggid sitting at the head of the table, dressed in clothes as he had been in his lifetime. The Maggid said to him: "You think I am not here, and you do whatever your heart desires. So I am showing you that I am sitting here." The rich man began to justify himself and make excuses, but the Maggid did not want to listen, and said to him: "Go away from me! Go away from me!" And he rebuked him: "Filet, take him home!" And Filet took him home. When they reached his house, Rabbi Filet said to him: "Go into your house in peace, for I have to go back." When the rich man went into his house, he fainted, and there was a great outcry, until his neighbors gathered and revived him. They asked him why he had fainted, and he told them the story. They said to him: "Let us go to Rabbi Avraham." They all went to Rabbi Avraham, and when he saw them, he laughed and said: "It seems my father called him." They said to him: "How do you know?" He answered: "Because my father told me he would call him."

**Summary:** Rabbi Avraham, the son of the Maggid of Mezritch, passed away after Yom Kippur, and Rabbi Nachum of Chernobyl wept over him greatly and spoke in his praise. Rabbi Avraham had told him that his father the Maggid, after his passing, would come to him and speak with him. Once, when a rich man caused Rabbi Avraham grief, the Maggid sent the servant Rabbi Filet, who was very far away, to bring the rich man at night to the study house, and there the rich man saw the Maggid sitting at the head of the table, and the Maggid rebuked him and sent him home.

## 41 — Rabbi Avraham as Sandek

Rabbi Nachum also told: Rabbi Avraham lived in the town of Fastov closed off and secluded, and the people of the town did not merit to see his face. Rabbi Nachum praised him before the people of the town, and they longed to see him. Once there was a circumcision. They gave Rabbi Nachum all the honors of the circumcision, and to Rabbi Avraham they gave the honor of being sandek. After the prayer they waited for him a long time, for he drew out his prayer. A great crowd gathered in the synagogue to see him. And when they said "Rabbi Avraham is coming to the synagogue," fear fell on them, and no more than about twenty men remained in the synagogue.

When he came to the synagogue (and his walking was a run), the rabbi of the community understood that he was going to his place, and moved aside into the corner. Rabbi Avraham came and sat in the rabbi's place, and rested his head on his hand, on the lectern. They brought the baby to the bimah, and Rabbi Avraham was still leaning on his hand. They said to the shammes: "Go and tell him that they have brought the baby," and the shammes would not go, out of great fear. They told others to go and tell him, and no one wanted to go. Meanwhile Rabbi Avraham himself came to the bimah.

Rabbi Nachum said: "I was so afraid when he came that the knife fell from my hand, and I caught the knife by the edge of the blade. And to this day I do not remember whether I said the blessings of the circumcision, from so much fear. I let him say the blessings over the *metzitzah*, and he said them." When Rabbi Nachum came home, he sat silent and wondering. His attendant brought him coffee to drink twice, and he did not want it. The attendant pressed him and begged him to drink it after all, and Rabbi Nachum answered him: "Fool! How can one drink coffee, when one hears that a man serves God with such service?"

**Summary:** Rabbi Avraham, the Maggid's son, lived in Fastov in seclusion, and the people of the town hardly ever saw him. When he was sandek at a circumcision, everyone was afraid of him, and Rabbi Nachum of Chernobyl, who was the mohel, was so afraid that the knife fell from his hand. Afterward he could not even drink coffee, for he said that one cannot drink when one hears that a man serves God with such service.

And when Rabbi Avraham said the blessing of the circumcision with great awe, the knife fell from his hand, from fear and trembling before the greatness of God. ## 43 — The Maggid's Daughter-in-Law

I heard from Rabbi Menachem, who heard it from the daughter-in-law of the great Maggid, the wife of Rabbi Avraham: when the Maggid's holy son, Rabbi Avraham, became a widower, the Maggid called two of the leading men of the town and said to them: "I ask you to travel to the town of Kremenets, and arrange a match for my son with the daughter of Rabbi Feivel the rabbi, the author of the book *Mishnat Chachamim*, and bring her here." At once they did as he commanded. They prepared a good wagon and horses for themselves, and also fine clothes, to do honor to the rabbi, and set out, until they came to Kremenets and stopped at an inn. When they had rested from the journey, right after the prayer, they went to Rabbi Feivel's house.

Rabbi Feivel's custom was not to engage in any business of making a living in the world, but only in Torah, all day and all night. Only his wife, the mistress of the house, saw to their livelihood. When they came to his house, his wife came back from the synagogue. She greeted them and said: "My brothers, where have you come from?" They said: "From Mezritch." She said: "What are you doing here? Some business, or something else?" And they told her that our great master had sent them to speak about her daughter, that she should marry his son, who had become a widower not long ago. The thing seemed laughable to her, for they did not know the rabbi, and had not heard of him. And besides, the girl was only twelve years old, and it had never entered their minds to look for matches for their daughter yet. After much talking, the words entered her ears. She said: "Thank God, my husband is at home. What do I care? Let him do as he wishes." They sent for him from the study house, and he came home. They put the matter before Rabbi Feivel, until the thing seemed right to him, and at once he ordered the scribe to come and write the engagement contract, in a good hour.

When they began to write the engagement contract, the messengers said: "Write whatever you like about the dowry. Only on the matter of the time of the wedding, it cannot possibly be put off." The bride's parents argued: "But our Sages said that a girl is given twelve months. And especially since we had not yet thought of marrying her off soon, and she has no clothes and other things ready." The messengers answered: "About that we are not particular. But it is impossible to put off the time of the wedding. She must travel with us at once." And since the thing had come from God, they agreed to this too. It was settled that Rabbi Feivel, so as not to interrupt his study, would stay at home, and the bride would travel with her mother to the wedding. They hired a good wagon and horses for them too, and they traveled with the messengers.

All the way the mother wondered at herself: what had happened to them, that they had let themselves be talked into this? Afterward they came to the town of Rovno, and all the people of the town came, from small to great, men, women and children, to welcome them and greet the bride of the great rabbi. And then her mind was set a little at rest. Afterward, when they reached Mezritch, men and children went out to meet them outside the town, until they had to drive slowly because of the great crowd that had come out to meet them. When they entered the town, all the women came out to meet them and greet them, until they came opposite the rabbi's house. The Maggid and his son came out to welcome them, and greeted them. Afterward they drove to their lodging. The wedding went on for the seven days of feasting with great joy, and words of Torah were said in abundance. After the wedding the bride's mother went home with great joy, for she had never seen or heard anything like it.

The Maggid drew his daughter-in-law very close. One day the daughter-in-law dreamed that she went into great halls, and there sat a Sanhedrin of honored elders, and they wanted to take her husband from her. She cried out before them, and made several claims before the court. She woke up, and it was a dream. But she did not tell anyone, and although she lived in the Maggid's house, she did not tell him. On the second night she dreamed the same dream again, and said nothing. And on the third night she dreamed it again, and pleaded much before them, until in the end they said to her: "See, because your claims are very just, we are giving him to you as a gift for twelve years." The next day, when she came to the Maggid in the morning, the Maggid praised her highly, and blessed her for her just claims, which had lengthened her husband's time by twelve years. And so it was.

After some years the Maggid went up to Heaven and passed away. Even so, in every trouble of theirs, or when they needed something, the Maggid would appear to her in a dream, and tell her what to do. Once, on Thursday night, he came to her in a dream and said: "Tell your husband to exchange his room for yours, or at least to put his books in your room." In the morning she told her husband, and he paid no attention to it, and said: "Why did he not tell me, and told you?" And because of our many sins his room burned down with the books.

Afterward Rabbi Avraham traveled about the region, and when he came to the town of Fastov, they received him with great honor, took him as their rabbi, and set a salary for him. And they sent a messenger to his wife, so that she too would come there for the Days of Awe. When they came to take her, the Maggid came to her in a dream and said to her: "Do not travel to Fastov in these wagons." In the morning she refused to go, and there was a great uproar among the tzaddikim Rabbi Meshulam Zusya and Rabbi Yehudah Leib HaKohen, for they wanted her to go there, to fulfill the will of the tzaddik. But she paid no attention to them. Not many days passed, and because of our many sins "the ark of God was hidden away" (the tzaddik passed away) there, in Fastov, and they sent a messenger to Anipoli. The tzaddikim were very ashamed before her, that the woman had seen better than they had. But they hid the matter from her, and revealed it only to her son, Rabbi Shalom the rabbi, who was small, so that he would say Kaddish, without revealing it to his mother.

But she, in her great wisdom, wondered at the eagerness of her son, who got up early in the morning to go to the synagogue, which he was not used to doing. When she went to the synagogue, she leaned against the wall of the study house, to hear whether her son was saying the mourner's Kaddish. She could not make it out exactly, until after the prayer she coaxed her son with words, and said: "Why are you hiding it from me? I heard from behind the wall that you said the mourner's Kaddish." Until he had to reveal the truth, and she mourned for her husband. After the seven days of mourning, she traveled to Fastov to receive what her husband had left. When she arrived there, they received her with great honor. In her honor they made a meal at her lodging that Sabbath, and all our fellow Hasidim ate there in her honor, to comfort her a little, but she would not be comforted.

At the third Sabbath meal almost all the people of the town gathered there, and sang the Sabbath songs as is the custom. And she, in the bitterness of her heart, sat sadly on the bed with the wife of the master of the house. She dozed off, and dreamed: great halls, beautiful and splendid. She opened a door, and her husband, Rabbi Avraham, came into the house shining like the sun at noon, very happy. After him came in several honored elders. He said to them: "Gentlemen, sit down." And they sat around the table. He said to them: "My wife has complaints against me about several things in which I acted with too much separation from the world, and she is right. And I ask her forgiveness before you." She said: "You are forgiven with a whole heart." And he also said before them: "The Torah has permitted her to marry another husband, especially since she is young, twenty-four years old, and I cannot prevent her. But if she agrees not to marry another, then all her needs are on me. And her sons, surely even before she gets home, each one of them will have a good position." And she woke up. And at once she no longer had an angry face, for she was comforted by his words.

When she came home, matches came at once for her sons and her daughters. God sent blessing on the work of her hands, and she made an honorable living from the shop she had, so that she lacked nothing. And in time of need her father-in-law, the Maggid, would appear to her, to tell her what to do.

After a long time the wife of the great rabbi Rabbi Menachem of Chernobyl died, and he wanted to take this woman as his wife. He took counsel with her son, Rabbi Shalom the rabbi, who was his son-in-law (his daughter's husband), and it seemed good to him too, for she was still young. Rabbi Menachem sent Rabbi Shalom to speak with his mother. On the way Rabbi Shalom dreamed: great halls, beautiful and splendid, and his father, the holy rabbi, was standing in the doorway of the hall, with both his hands on the roof, and crying out in a loud voice: "Who is he, and where is he, whose heart has dared to enter my hall?" He woke up, and understood how far the matter reached, and went back home in peace.

**Summary:** The Maggid of Mezritch sent messengers to Kremenets to arrange a match for his widowed son, Rabbi Avraham, with a girl of twelve, and insisted that she come at once, and all the towns on the way came out to receive her with honor. The bride dreamed that a court in Heaven wanted to take her husband, and because of her claims they gave him twelve more years, and even after the Maggid's death he appeared to her in dreams and warned her. After her husband's passing she saw him in a dream asking her forgiveness and promising to provide for her and her children if she did not remarry, and when Rabbi Menachem of Chernobyl wanted to marry her, her son dreamed that his father was crying out: "Who dared to enter my hall?"

## 44 — Rabbi Aaron of Karlin and the Tefillin

The great Maggid, Rabbi Dov of Mezritch, once commanded his disciple, Rabbi Aaron of Karlin, to travel home by way of a certain community. Rabbi Aaron did as he commanded. When he came to that town, he searched his bag, and saw that he had forgotten the bag with his tallit and tefillin. And Rabbi Aaron had never prayed in a borrowed tallit and tefillin. He sent to a shop to buy a tallit and to put tzitzit on it, and he himself went to the scribe to buy tefillin. When the scribe put the tefillin into his hand, Rabbi Aaron at once cried out: "Yesterday you sinned with a married woman, and today you sell tefillin? Wicked man, confess!" And the scribe confessed his sin. After he confessed, they made inquiries and investigations, and found many men and women who had all been defiled by sins, until more than a hundred bills of divorce were needed. In one matter Rabbi Aaron had a question, and he did not know what to do, and he had to go back to the great Maggid. <!-- MATURE: adultery across a town, more than a hundred divorces -->

When he came, the Maggid told him everything that had happened, and also the question about which he was in doubt, and what the law was in practice. Before Rabbi Aaron traveled to that town again, the Maggid said to him: "Let Rabbi Menachem Mendel, who knows how to speak Polish, go with you. And also take your white Sabbath clothes with you." And so they did. On the way Rabbi Menachem Mendel said: "Our journey is only to arrange the bills of divorce. If so, why is it necessary to speak Polish, and white clothes too? It can only be that our master sees with the eye of the mind that the wicked have regretted confessing, and have informed on us to the lord of the town." And so it was. When they reached the town, the lord posted guards to bring them to him at once, and he wanted to do them harm. The wicked hid with the lord in another room.

When they came to the lord, dressed in white clothes, they found favor in his eyes. He received them with great honor, and told them to sit beside him. In the course of the conversation the lord asked them: "Why have these rabbis come here?" Rabbi Menachem Mendel said: "Rabbi Aaron is a man of God, and he saw that there are great accusations against your town, that literally destruction has been decreed on it, because the people of the town are evil and sin greatly against God, especially in matters of adultery. Many women must be divorced, and we have come to you, for you are the lord of the town, and you must see to their good." As they spoke to him, the lord remembered the words of the wicked. He ordered them to come out and speak with the rabbis. At once Rabbi Aaron thundered at them with a voice of noise and anger, and they had to confess all the sins before the lord. The lord ordered them to carry out the divorces, and gave the rabbis great authority.

**Summary:** The Maggid of Mezritch sent Rabbi Aaron of Karlin to a certain town, and there, when he bought tefillin, he sensed at once that the scribe had sinned with a married woman, and the scribe confessed. So it came out that many in the town had sinned, and more than a hundred bills of divorce were needed. When Rabbi Aaron went back to the town, the Maggid sent with him Rabbi Menachem Mendel, who knew Polish, and white clothes, because the sinners had informed on them to the lord. The lord was impressed by the rabbis, the sinners confessed before him, and the lord ordered the bills of divorce to be arranged.
