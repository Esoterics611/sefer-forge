## The Author's Introduction

I want to explain to the reader why I decided to write this book, so that he will not wonder at it. There are people who mock and say: "If you had kept silent, your silence would have been finer than your words." But there is no real claim in what they say; it is only mockery in another style.

God is my witness that I have no intention of boasting, or of looking great, or of making a name for myself in the world.

I will reveal a little of my reasons. I heard from the great rabbi, Rabbi Gedaliah, in the name of the pious Rabbi Shmeril, the preacher of the town of Verkhivka, words about the famous signs, wonders and miracles, and about how a person must hold on to faith.

I too have noticed it: from the time I became a man until I grew old, I see that because of our sins the miracles and wonders grow fewer from day to day. In earlier times it sometimes happened that a person lay in a faint, like a dead man, and afterward got up and came back to life, and told of the awesome things he had seen in the upper world. So it is told in the Gemara too ("I saw a clear world"), and in other stories in the Gemara.

For example, my father-in-law, Rabbi Alexander, told that in the town of Bershtat a certain man lay in such a faint. This was at the time when the sect of Shabbetai Tzvi was stirring. They showed this man places in books where some rabbis had gone wrong, rabbis who had almost been drawn after the sect, and they commanded him to tell them the true meaning of those places.

In his days, too, there were madmen who struck themselves with stones during the Torah reading. They revealed people's sins to them, and told about themselves for which sin they were being punished in the "hollow of the sling" (a punishment in which the soul is hurled from place to place, like a stone in a sling).

I remember that in my youth I studied in a village with a teacher. Poor people came to the cheder, and they were given food. Among them was a woman into whom an evil spirit had entered, and nobody knew of it. When the teacher began to study the Five Books of Moses with the children, he managed to study only two or three verses, and the evil spirit threw the woman to the ground. Her husband came and asked the teacher to stop studying, because the impure spirit in her could not bear anything holy. When the teacher stopped, the woman got up and sat down again in her place.

Because of all these things, many people repented, and faith grew stronger in the heart of every Jew.

But today, because of our sins, the righteous are few, faith has sunk very low, and much heresy has spread in the world. May God forgive it.

So I took the advice to write down the awesome stories I heard from truthful men. In each story I wrote from whom I heard it. Thank God, who gave me a good memory. I added nothing and took nothing away; it is all true, and nothing changed as I told it.

I wrote all this as a remembrance for my children and my grandchildren, to strengthen in them faith in God and in His Torah, faith in the righteous, and faith in everyone who draws himself close to God and to the Torah; and so that they will see how the Torah purifies the souls of those who study it, and how a person can reach high levels, as our Sages said: "A person should always hope and say: When will my deeds reach the deeds of my fathers Abraham, Isaac and Jacob?"

Therefore everyone who reads this should know: I did not write it in order to tell stories or to write history. I wrote it so that the reader would reflect with his mind on each and every deed, see in it the wonders of God, and learn from it a moral lesson for himself: to bind his heart to the fear of God, to faith in the sages, and to the power of the holy Torah.

I wanted to write after each story what moral lesson and what faith one learns from it. But I was afraid that I would not write it well enough, and so I left it to each reader to learn from the story according to his own level: the small one according to his smallness, and the great one according to his greatness.

And I said to myself: together with my children, perhaps I will merit to give this to the rest of the Jewish people as well. May God help me to be among those who bring merit to the Jewish people. I ask my descendants to give the book to everyone. And to everyone who reads it I will tell things from days of old, as it is written in Psalms: "That which we have heard and known, and our fathers have told us, we will not hide from their children... telling the praises of the Lord... so that the last generation may know."

"A watcher and a holy one came down from heaven" (a holy angel came down from heaven, in the words of the Book of Daniel). Such was Rabbi Israel Baal Shem Tov, the light of the world, the holy lamp, together with his companions. He was a spark of the soul of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai. So I heard from the son of Rabbi Yaakov of Mezhibuzh, who was called Rabbi Yakil: once his father took him to the study house, and the Baal Shem Tov was standing and praying before the ark. His father said to him: "My son, look and see, for a thing like this will not be seen in the world until the Messiah comes. This is Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai and his companions."

And one sees it also from the story told later in the book, with his brother-in-law Rabbi Gershon of Kitov: during the afternoon prayer the soul of the Baal Shem Tov would rise on high in order to raise up many souls. In the book *Etz Chaim* (in the Gate of the Sabbath, chapter 8) it is written that Moses our teacher was quick to do this every Sabbath eve: to raise up all the souls, of the living and of the dead. From this it appears that the Baal Shem Tov also had something of the soul of Moses our teacher. See the story itself; and what is known needs no proof.

**Summary:** The author explains that he wrote the book because miracles are growing fewer and faith is growing weaker. He writes that he wrote down only what he heard from truthful men, without adding or taking away, and that he left it to each reader to learn the moral lesson for himself. At the end he says that the Baal Shem Tov was a spark of the soul of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, and that he also had something of the soul of Moses our teacher.

## 1 — The Baal Shem Tov's Father, and the Boy Who Was Not Afraid

The printer says: In the manuscripts I copied from the author, the story of the Baal Shem Tov's family and of how he became known was not written in order. But I heard all of it in the name of our master (the Rebbe, the Rav), in the right order and with a good explanation. So I will print it as they heard it from his holy mouth, and what branches out from it I will write afterward, as it is written in the manuscripts.

The Baal Shem Tov's father, Rabbi Eliezer, lived in the land of Wallachia, near the border. He and his wife were old. Once robbers came to the town and took him captive. His wife fled to another town, and because of her great poverty she became a midwife, and that is how she made her living. The captors took Rabbi Eliezer to a distant land, where there were no Jews, and sold him there.

Rabbi Eliezer served his master faithfully. The master loved him and put him in charge of his whole household. Rabbi Eliezer asked his master for permission to rest on the Sabbath, and the master agreed. After a long time Rabbi Eliezer wanted to escape and save himself, but in a dream he was told: "Do not hurry. You still need to be in this land."

One day the master had business with the viceroy (the king's chief adviser), and he gave him Rabbi Eliezer as a gift, and praised him highly. The viceroy too loved him at once, and gave him a room of his own. Rabbi Eliezer did not have to work at all, except for one thing: when the viceroy came back from the king, Rabbi Eliezer went out to meet him with a basin of water to wash his feet, as was the custom of great noblemen. All the rest of the time he sat in his room and occupied himself with Torah and prayer.

Once the king had a great war. He called his adviser so that they could plan together how to fight, but the matter was difficult, and they could not work out what to do. The king was very angry at the viceroy, who did not know how to help in a time of trouble. The viceroy went home angry. When he arrived, Rabbi Eliezer came out to meet him with the basin of water as usual, but the viceroy refused it, and lay down on his bed with an angry face. Rabbi Eliezer said to him: "My lord, why are you angry? Tell me." And the viceroy scolded him.

But Rabbi Eliezer was a faithful servant and wanted his master's good, so he risked himself and came to him again and again, until the viceroy was forced to tell him what had happened. Rabbi Eliezer said to him: "Interpretations belong to God, and the Lord is a man of war. I will fast for several days in a row, and I will ask God about this secret, for He reveals secrets." He asked a "dream question" (he asked Heaven to answer him in a dream), and they answered him exactly how the war should be fought, all of it in detail and clearly.

The next day he came to his master and told him all the advice he had received from Heaven. The viceroy was overjoyed. He went at once to the king in great joy and said to him: "My lord the king, this is the advice." And he told him everything. When the king heard his words, he said: "This is wonderful advice. This is not human intelligence. It comes either from a man of God, on whom a higher spirit rests, or the opposite, from the spirit of impurity. And I know you, and I know that you are certainly not a man of God. If so, you are a sorcerer!"

The viceroy was forced to admit the truth and tell the king the whole story.

> (In the manuscripts the story is written like this: Once the king went out with his soldiers in ships to fight against a fortress. When they arrived, the fortress looked small to him. The king said: "It is already late in the day, and the fortress is small. We will spend the night here, a little way off, and in the morning we will not need to trouble the whole army; we will take it with a few soldiers." Rabbi Eliezer was then a sailor on one of the ships. In a dream he was told to go to the king and tell him not to make light of this battle, or else he and his whole army would be lost: in the sea, all around the city, there are iron pillars, and the city cannot be reached by ship, because the ships would overturn at once. It can be reached only through the city's entrance. And in the dream they also revealed to him the right way, with clear signs, so that they would not go wrong.
>
> In the morning the king rose early with the whole camp, and they began to wake the sailors to sail the ships. Rabbi Eliezer refused to sail, and said: "I have a secret matter to tell the king." At once they shaved him, changed his clothes, and brought him to the king in a small boat. He told the king what had been revealed to him from Heaven, and said: "If the king does not believe it, let him send a small boat with men who have been sentenced to death, and we will see what happens to them." The king did so. When the boat reached the place of the pillars, it overturned, and all the men in it were lost. The king said: "If so, give advice: what should we do?" Rabbi Eliezer said to him: "There is a straight way, the city's entrance, where the people of the city go in and out." And he showed him the signs. They did so, and the king succeeded and captured the fortress. Here ends the account in the manuscripts.)

The king made Rabbi Eliezer commander of his army, for he saw with open eyes that God was with him and that everything he did succeeded. And wherever the king sent him to fight, he was very successful. Meanwhile Rabbi Eliezer thought: "What will happen in the end? Perhaps now is the time to escape home." And again it was revealed to him from Heaven: "You still need to be in this land."

One day the viceroy died, and the king appointed Rabbi Eliezer in his place to be his adviser, because he loved him. He also gave him the viceroy's daughter as his wife. But Rabbi Eliezer, with God's help, did not come close to her as a husband to his wife. He looked for all kinds of excuses not to be at home, and even when he was at home, he did not come near her at all. In that land a Jew was forbidden to live, and a Jew who was caught there was sentenced to death. So several years passed. Once his wife said to him: "Tell me, what fault have you found in me, that you do not come near me and do not behave with me as every husband does with his wife?" He said to her: "Swear to me that you will not tell anyone, and I will tell you the truth." She swore, and he said to her: "I am a Jew." At once she sent him back to his own country, with a great deal of money and gold.

But on the way robbers attacked him and took everything he had. On the way Elijah the Prophet appeared to him and said: "Because of what you did, a son will be born to you who will light up the eyes of Israel, and the verse 'Israel, in whom I will be glorified' will come true in him." Rabbi Eliezer reached home and found his wife, thank God, alive. And in their old age the Baal Shem Tov was born to them, when both of them were close to a hundred years old. (The Baal Shem Tov said that his soul could not be brought down into the world until desire had come completely to an end.)

The child grew and was weaned. When the time came for his father to die, he took the child in his arms and said: "I see that you will light my lamp, and I have not merited to raise you. But my beloved son, remember one thing all your days: God is with you. And so do not be afraid of anything." (In the name of our master I heard a natural explanation of why these words were engraved in his heart: our Sages said that what a child says, he heard from his father or his mother, and especially a "child of old age," born to his parents when they were old. Jacob loved Joseph because he was the child of his old age, and so the bond between them was very great, as is written in the holy Zohar. And so it was here too: because of the great and true bond between them, the words were engraved in the heart of the Baal Shem Tov, even though he was still a very small child.)

After his father passed away, the child grew up. The people of the town had greatly respected his father, so they were kind to him and sent him to study with a teacher. He did very well in his studies, but he had a habit: he would study for a few days and then run away from the cheder. They searched for him and found him sitting alone in the forest. They thought this was because he was an orphan with no one to watch over him, and so he was a wild boy, and they brought him back to the teacher. This happened several times: he ran away to the forest to be alone there.

Little by little the matter was forgotten, and they gave up sending him to the teacher. The boy grew up unlike the other children. He was hired as a *belfer*, the teacher's helper: he brought the little children to cheder, and took them to the synagogue to answer "Amen, may His great name be blessed," the Kedushah and "Amen" in a sweet voice. This was his holy service, with the schoolchildren, whose breath is pure of sin. All along the way he sang with them in a sweet voice and with great eagerness, and he could be heard from far away. His service rose very high and gave great delight in Heaven, like the song the Levites sang in the Holy Temple. It was a time of great favor in Heaven.

But the Satan came among them too, for he understood how far these things reached, and he feared for himself that the time would come when he would vanish from the world. The Satan entered into a certain sorcerer. When the Baal Shem Tov was walking with the children, singing with eagerness and in a sweet voice, the sorcerer turned into a wild beast called a *vukilak* (a kind of werewolf), and attacked them. They all fled in fear, and some of the children even fell ill. And so this regular service came to a stop.

After a while the Baal Shem Tov remembered his father's words, not to be afraid of anything because God was with him, and he grew strong in his faith in God. He went to the householders, the children's fathers, and spoke to their hearts so that they would bring the children back to him: "I will stand against the beast and kill it in God's name. Why should the holy service of the children stop because of it, when it is such a great thing?" And they listened to him. He took a good, strong stick in his hand. When he was walking with the children in song and joy, the beast attacked them. He ran at it, struck it on the forehead, and it died. The next day they found the gentile sorcerer lying dead on the ground.

From then on he became the watchman of the study house. And this was his custom: as long as the scholars in the study house were awake, he slept. When they went to sleep, he woke up and did his service in full, until the time came when people wake up, and then he went back to sleep. Everyone thought he slept the whole night, from beginning to end.

**Summary:** Rabbi Eliezer, the Baal Shem Tov's father, was taken captive to a distant land, rose to greatness with the king through advice he received from Heaven, and kept his Judaism until he was set free. Elijah the Prophet promised him a son, and the Baal Shem Tov was born when both his parents were nearly a hundred. Before his death his father told him: "God is with you; do not be afraid of anything." When a sorcerer turned into a wild beast and frightened the children, the boy remembered his father's words and killed the beast, and afterward he became the watchman of the study house and served God in secret at night.

## 2 — The Writings of Rabbi Adam, and the Secret Wedding

> (I heard from Rabbi Shimshon, the rabbi of Rashkov, son of the Rabbi of Polonne, a story about a man named Rabbi Adam. It was from him that the writings reached the Baal Shem Tov: once Rabbi Adam went into a cave, and found there writings of the secrets of the Torah.
>
> Once Rabbi Adam invited the emperor to a feast, and the emperor promised to come. Rabbi Adam lived in a very small house. And the feast came about like this: he saw that one king was preparing a feast for another king. The preparations went on for two years, so that in that time great palaces could be built. They built upper stories of beautiful, splendid crystal glass, and over the stories ran a channel of water, with fish swimming back and forth in it. They prepared credenzas of silver and gold, food, drink and servants, as befits the honor of a king, and set a day when the visiting king would come to the feast. And for that very day Rabbi Adam invited the emperor. And the emperor traveled with all his ministers to Rabbi Adam, to the feast.
>
> Among the ministers was one who hated the Jews very much. He tried to persuade the emperor not to go, because he would be put to shame when it turned out that Rabbi Adam had neither a palace nor a feast. The emperor did not listen to him and went. On the way the minister again tried to persuade him to go back to the imperial court, and again the emperor did not listen. Even so, the emperor sent a minister to see whether Rabbi Adam had prepared anything for the feast, and whether there was room in his house. The messenger came back and said that he had a small house, as before. Even so, the emperor went on. When he came near the town, he sent another messenger, and he came back with the same answer. But when the emperor reached the street where Rabbi Adam lived, he saw great palaces and a great courtyard. He went into the courtyard with his ministers and servants. At once the servants took all the horses to the stables, and each servant did his job, but they were all mute. They ate and drank of all good things. Rabbi Adam said to them: "Eat and drink as much as you like, only let none of you take any vessel from here."
>
> Afterward Rabbi Adam said: "Let each one say what he wants, 'This is the thing I want,' and put his hand into the pocket of his garment, and he will find the thing there." The emperor began, said what he wanted, and found it in the pocket of his garment. And all the ministers did the same. When the turn came of the minister who hated the Jews, he too said something, and Rabbi Adam said to him: "Put your hand into the pocket of your garment." When he put his hand into the pocket, it got dirty, and he pulled it out smeared with excrement, with a great stench. The emperor ordered him thrown out. The minister washed his hand in water, and it did not help. He began to plead with Rabbi Adam. Rabbi Adam said to him: "If you take it upon yourself not to hate the Jews anymore, well and good. And if not, your hands will be dirty all the days of your life." The minister took it upon himself. Rabbi Adam said to him: "There is no remedy for you unless a Jew urinates on your hand. With that you will wash, and that will help you." And so it was. <!-- MATURE: crude bodily content (excrement on the minister's hand; cured by a Jew urinating on it) -->
>
> The emperor took two cups from one of the credenzas, and hid them in his garment. After the emperor left, the palaces vanished, and of all the things only the two cups were missing, and what they had eaten and drunk. The newspapers of the world wrote of the wonder: the palaces had vanished with all the preparations, and afterward they came back whole; only two cups were missing, and what had been eaten and drunk. When the newspapers reached the emperor, he sent a letter to that king, and wrote in it: "I have a Jew here who brought us your palace, and we ate the feast you prepared. As a sign, I am sending you your two cups."
>
> Once Rabbi Adam's wife said to him: "How long will we suffer this poverty?" He said to her: "What do you want? Whatever garment you like, go to the storeroom and take a coat from the rod, whichever you want." She went to the storeroom, took a coat from the rod as she wished, and brought it home. But Rabbi Adam urged her to return it, saying: "It is better for us to suffer poverty in this world." And she did so, and returned the coat. Rabbi Adam asked a dream question: to whom should he hand over his writings? And they answered him: "Hand them over to Rabbi Israel son of Eliezer, of the town of Okup.")

Before his death Rabbi Adam commanded his only son: "I have writings of the secrets of the Torah, but you are not worthy of them. Look for a town called Okup. There you will find a man named Israel son of Eliezer, who is about fourteen years old. Hand the writings over to him, for they belong to the root of his soul. And if you merit that he will study with you, how good that will be."

After Rabbi Adam passed away, his only son, who was very great in Torah and perfect in every virtue, took a wagon and horses and traveled from town to town until he came to Okup. He stayed with the *parnas* of the month (the man who ran the community that month). The parnas asked him: "Where are you from and where are you going? For by the look of it, you have not come to collect money." He answered him: "My late father was a famous tzaddik, and before his passing he commanded me to marry here, in Okup, and I must carry out all his words." At once the whole town was astir, and many matches were offered to him, for everyone who saw him loved him. He made a match with the family of a certain rich man.

After the wedding he began to look for the man he was seeking, and found no one except Israel, the caretaker of the study house. When he looked at him closely, it seemed to him that he was not what he appeared to be from the outside, and that perhaps this was the man he was looking for. He said to his father-in-law: "Your house is noisy, and it is hard for me to study. Do me a favor: build me a small room in the study house, so that I can study there in peace. As our Sages said: 'Where there is song, there let there be prayer.'" His intention was to be near Israel all the time, so as to get to know him deeply. The father-in-law did it without delay, for his son-in-law was very dear to him. He also ordered the Baal Shem Tov to serve his son-in-law, and paid him for it.

One night, when everyone was asleep, the rabbi's son pretended that he too was asleep. The Baal Shem Tov rose as was his custom to study Torah and serve God, and the rabbi's son watched him once and again. On the third night the Baal Shem Tov rose and studied, and in the middle of it he fell asleep. The rabbi's son got up, took one booklet of the writings, laid it before him, and again pretended to be asleep. When the Baal Shem Tov woke and saw the booklet, he trembled greatly, studied it, and hid it in his clothes. The rabbi's son did the same on another night too, until he saw clearly that this was the man to whom his father had commanded him to hand over the writings. He called him and said to him: "My father left me these writings, and commanded me to hand them over to you. Here are all the writings before you. But I ask a great favor of you: that you study with me." The Baal Shem Tov answered him: "Good, but on condition that no one knows of it except you, and that you show no sign of it. On the contrary, go on ordering me to serve you as before." And so they did.

Afterward the rabbi's son said to his father-in-law: "Now I want more seclusion. If possible, get me a house of my own outside the town, and I will sit there over Torah and the service of God." And the father-in-law did so. He asked his father-in-law to hire the boy Israel to live with him, to keep him company, and to serve him whenever he needed to send him somewhere. And the father-in-law did so, for his son-in-law was greatly respected. When the people of the community saw that Israel was studying with the rabbi's son, they said: "Surely his father's merit caused the rabbi's son to come here and draw him close," for it seemed to them that now he was going in a good way. They found him a wife, but after a short time his wife died.

In the house of seclusion they studied everything that was needed: Gemara, Rashi, Tosafot, the codes of law, and also all the holy writings. In the writings there was divine Kabbalah, and also "practical Kabbalah" (using holy names to act in the world). Once the rabbi's son asked the Baal Shem Tov to bring down the "Prince of the Torah" (the angel of the Torah), so that he would explain something to him. The Baal Shem Tov put him off, saying: "If we make a mistake in the mystical intention, there may be danger, for we have no ashes of the red heifer (with which one is purified)." The rabbi's son urged him day after day, until he could not refuse. They fasted from Sabbath to Sabbath without a break, immersed in the mikveh properly, and at the end of the Sabbath they concentrated on the known mystical intentions. At once the Baal Shem Tov cried out: "Oh, oh, we made a mistake! The Prince of Fire has come down, and he will surely burn the whole town! So you, whom everyone holds to be a very pious man, go quickly to your father-in-law and to the whole town, so that they save themselves, for the town will soon burn." And so it was.

From then on everyone held the rabbi's son to be a man of God and a wonder-worker. After a long time he again urged the Baal Shem Tov to bring down the Prince of the Torah. The Baal Shem Tov refused, but the rabbi's son urged him for several days, until he was forced to do what he wanted. They fasted as the time before, and at the end of the Sabbath, when they concentrated on the known mystical intentions, the Baal Shem Tov cried out: "Alas! Because of this, death has been decreed on both of us tonight. But we have a remedy: if we stay strong and stand all night and concentrate on the mystical intentions, without tasting the least sleep, the judgment will be canceled. But if one of us dozes off, then, since sleep comes from the side of death, the accuser will get a hold to rule over him completely." So they did all night. Before daybreak the rabbi's son could not hold out, and dozed off a little. When the Baal Shem Tov saw this, he ran quickly and made a great noise. The rabbi's son suddenly fainted away. They tried to wake him, and it did not help. They buried him with great honor.

Afterward the Baal Shem Tov took another path. He left the place, went to the community near Brody, and became a teacher there. Everyone loved him, for he was a very great scholar and very wise, so that all the affairs of the community were decided by him. When people had a quarrel and came to him, he brought the law to light, and both sides, the one in the right and the one in the wrong, were pleased with him, because in his great wisdom he spoke to their hearts so that it would seem good to everyone.

Rabbi Gershon of Kitov was the head of the rabbinical court in Brody. His father, Rabbi Avraham, had a lawsuit with a man of the community where the Baal Shem Tov was. Rabbi Avraham came there and told his opponent to travel with him to Brody, to settle the dispute by Torah law. The man said to him: "We have a teacher here who is great in Torah, and he is a righteous judge. Every time a case comes before him, both sides are pleased, because he explains the ruling well. Let us go to him and put our claims before him, and if you are not satisfied, I will travel with you to Brody." Rabbi Avraham agreed, and they came before him.

The moment Rabbi Avraham came before the Baal Shem Tov, the Baal Shem Tov saw with the holy spirit that Rabbi Avraham's daughter was his destined match. In those days it was the custom of great scholars, when an important guest came to them, to say before him an explanation of the Torah. The Baal Shem Tov said before him a very sharp explanation of Maimonides, and several more explanations, until Rabbi Avraham's soul was drawn after him and bound to his soul. Afterward the two sides presented their claims. There were many claims and quarrels between them, and within about an hour the Baal Shem Tov brought out the true judgment. Rabbi Avraham was greatly amazed, and loved him greatly.

Meanwhile Rabbi Avraham learned that the Baal Shem Tov was unmarried and needed to marry. Rabbi Avraham had a divorced daughter. He came to him in secret and said to him: "I have heard that you need a wife. Perhaps you will agree to marry my daughter?" The Baal Shem Tov answered him: "Good. But in this community many rich men want me as a son-in-law, so the matter must be kept secret, so that no one knows. I could not face them, after they have always been good to me and honored me. If you agree, we will write the engagement contract in secret. And I ask one more thing of you: make the match with me, not with my Torah and my wisdom. I do not want you to write any praises or virtues about me at all, only 'Israel son of Eliezer.'" Because Rabbi Avraham's soul was bound to his soul, he agreed to everything he said. They wrote the engagement contract without titles, and without writing the name of the place, and no one knew of it.

On the way home Rabbi Avraham fell ill with the illness from which he died, and he passed away. They sent word to his son, the great Rabbi Gershon of Kitov, in Brody, and he came to eulogize his father as is proper. He received all the papers that were found among his father's things, and among them he found the engagement contract: his father had matched his daughter, Rabbi Gershon's sister, with a man named Israel. Rabbi Gershon was very surprised: his father was a famous man; how had he made a match with a simple man, and with a "so-and-so" at that, of whom no one knew where he was from or what family he came from? He told his sister about it. She said to him: "If our father saw him as suitable, we have nothing to doubt."

The Baal Shem Tov waited until his term of work as a teacher was over. He came to the householder and said: "I am going back to my place." They spoke to his heart and offered him a higher salary to stay with them, but he did not agree, for that was not his intention. He disguised himself: he changed his clothes to look like one of the good-for-nothings, in a short fur coat and a wide belt, changed his manners and his speech, and went to Brody to the house of Rabbi Gershon. Two judges sat before Rabbi Gershon, and he stood beside them, looking all the time into the cases that came before them. The Baal Shem Tov stood by the door. Rabbi Gershon thought he was a poor man, took a coin and wanted to give it to him. The Baal Shem Tov said to him: "I have a secret matter to tell you." He went with him into a room, showed him the engagement contract, and said to him: "Give me my wife." When Rabbi Gershon saw the man, his clothes and his speech, he was very frightened and astonished: what had his father done? He called his sister and told her everything. She answered him: "If our father did this, we must not doubt him. Surely it comes from God, and perhaps good offspring will come from him." And so they did, and set the time of the wedding.

Before the wedding canopy, the Baal Shem Tov said that he wanted first to speak with his wife, as our Sages said. He spoke with her in secret, and revealed the truth to her, all of it. He made her swear not to reveal anything of it, even if many times of great poverty should pass over her.

After the wedding Rabbi Gershon wanted to study with him; perhaps he would take in a little Torah. But the Baal Shem Tov hid himself, as if he were unable to take in anything at all. Rabbi Gershon said to his sister: "The truth is that your husband is a great disgrace to me. If you want to divorce him, well and good. And if not, I will buy you a horse, and you will go with him to live wherever you want, for I cannot bear this disgrace." She agreed. They set out, and the Baal Shem Tov chose a place for her to live, and he himself went to seclude himself among great mountains. And this was her livelihood: two or three times a week she came to him with a horse and wagon, he dug clay, and she carted it to the town, and from this she made her living.

The Baal Shem Tov always fasted long fasts. When he wanted to eat, he dug a hollow in the ground, put flour and water in it, and the heat of the sun baked it. That was all his food after a fast, and all his days he lived in seclusion. The mountains were very high, and between them was a deep valley, as steep as a smooth wall. Once the Baal Shem Tov was deep in contemplation, and walked on the mountains. On the other mountain stood robbers. They saw him from afar walking to the edge of the mountain, lost in thought, and said: "He will surely fall into the valley and be dashed to pieces." But as he came near the valley, the other mountain came near to him and became level ground, and he crossed over; and afterward the two mountains moved apart as before. And when he came back, as he came near the valley, the first mountain came near the second and became level ground. And so it happened several times, going and coming back.

The robbers saw that he was a man of God, that God was with him. They made peace with him, came before him and told him all they had seen, and said: "We have seen with our own eyes that you are a man of God. So we ask you to pray for us, that God make our way succeed, for we always go on it at the risk of our lives." The Baal Shem Tov said to them: "If you swear to me that you will not harm a Jew or rob him, I will do as you ask." They swore to it. From then on, when they had a quarrel or a claim among themselves, they came before him, and he made a compromise between them.

Once two robbers came before him for judgment, and one of them was not satisfied with the ruling. He said in his heart: "When the Baal Shem Tov is awake, no harm can be done to him. But when he is asleep, I will do as I wish and kill him." And so he did: when the Baal Shem Tov was asleep, the robber came and raised his axe to cut off his head. At once he was seized, he did not know by whom, and beaten with cruel blows, until there was hardly any life left in him and he had no strength to speak. The Baal Shem Tov woke, and saw before him the beaten man, covered with blood and wounds. He asked him: "Who are you, and who did this to you?" And he had no strength to answer. And the Baal Shem Tov went on his way. The next day, when he had recovered a little, the robber told him everything that had happened.

**Summary:** Rabbi Adam, a wonder-worker who found writings of the secrets of the Torah in a cave, commanded his son to hand them over to the boy Israel son of Eliezer of Okup. The son found the Baal Shem Tov, who was hiding as a simple caretaker, and studied with him, but he died after he urged him to bring down the "Prince of the Torah" and could not stay awake all night. Afterward Rabbi Avraham secretly matched his daughter with the Baal Shem Tov, and her brother, Rabbi Gershon, thought he was a simple man and sent the couple away from the town. The Baal Shem Tov secluded himself among the mountains, and robbers who saw a miracle done for him swore not to harm Jews.
